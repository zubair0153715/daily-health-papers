## Abstract

Chronic pain is the leading cause of disability worldwide — about 619 million people lived with low back pain in 2020 — yet the analgesic ladder runs from NSAIDs that fail in neuropathic pain to opioids that carry dependence liability. TRPV1, the capsaicin receptor on pain-sensing nerve endings, is the non-opioid target: capsaicin itself works only topically, as the 8% patch, while every systemic TRPV1 antagonist that reached humans failed — AMG-517 for hyperthermia past 40 °C. We asked whether known antagonists and ChEMBL similarity leads bind the human TRPV1 vanilloid pocket more strongly than re-docked capsaicin. Using the LeadForge pipeline (UniProtKB Q8NER1 → ChEMBL CHEMBL4794 → PubChem/RDKit, PAINS and ADMET screen → AutoDock Vina 1.2.7), we docked ten compounds on 8GFA, the 2.29 Å human TRPV1 structure with the antagonist SB-366791, centred exactly on its co-crystallised ligand (centre audit by direct PDB computation — the pipeline's own centre picker lands on a phospholipid 18.4 Å from the pocket). Capsaicin re-docked at −8.26 kcal/mol and SB-366791 self-docked at −9.36 kcal/mol, validating the protocol. AMG-517 scored best at −10.45 kcal/mol, ahead of capsaicin by 2.19, beyond Vina's ±1–2 kcal/mol noise; SB-705498 scored −9.91 and three pipeline leads scored −9.65 to −9.73, all ahead of capsaicin but inside noise. The honest limit: the best binder is the drug that failed in Phase I for fever, the ranking rests on a single antagonist-bound structure, resiniferatoxin failed to dock, and a docking score cannot tell an agonist from an antagonist. The falsifiable next step is a calcium-flux assay of AMG-517 and SB-705498 against capsaicin-evoked human TRPV1 activation, with proton-mode selectivity and core-temperature monitoring: if AMG-517's IC50 is not within 3-fold of SB-705498's published 3 nM, or if it blocks the proton mode as potently as the capsaicin mode, the docking ranking is refuted as a drug lead.

## 1. Introduction

Chronic pain is the largest single cause of disability on earth. The Global Burden of Disease 2021 analysis counted about 619 million people living with low back pain in 2020 — the leading cause of years lived with disability worldwide — and projects about 843 million cases by 2050. In Pakistan, where most pain care is paid for out of pocket, the standard analgesic ladder runs from paracetamol and NSAIDs, which fail in neuropathic pain and injure stomach and kidney with chronic use, to opioids, which carry dependence liability. A non-opioid target that works peripherally, without reaching the brain's reward circuits, is the gap this paper attacks.

That target is transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1; gene TRPV1, chromosome 17p13.2; UniProt Q8NER1; 839 amino acids): a non-selective, calcium-permeant cation channel in the free nerve endings of primary pain-sensing Aδ and C fibres. It is a polymodal integrator — opened by noxious heat above about 42 °C, by protons, and by vanilloids — and it mediates inflammatory pain and hyperalgesia. Its genetics and pharmacology are unusually clean for a pain target: the channel was cloned as the capsaicin receptor (Hayes et al., Pain 2000;88:205–215; PMID 11050376), and its activation by capsaicin is selective — capsaicin does not activate the homologous TRPV2–6 channels.

The reference drug of this paper is capsaicin itself — and the paper states its route plainly, because the route is the point. Capsaicin is a TOPICAL drug, not an oral systemic one. It activates TRPV1 with an EC50 of approximately 700 nM, and the 8% cutaneous patch (Qutenza) works by a paradox: a single 60-minute application over-activates the channel until the nerve ending desensitises, giving months of relief. In the pivotal trials a single application cut pain by 22.8–32.3% against 10.7–25.0% for control patches, with 34–47% of patients reaching a 30% response against 18–36% on control, and retreatment every 90 days (EMA product information; Backonja et al., Lancet Neurol 2008;7:1106–1112 — 402 patients with postherpetic neuralgia, −29.6% against −19.9% at weeks 2–8, 42% against 32% responders). Only about 1% of the applied capsaicin is absorbed into the skin. The drug's weakness is therefore not efficacy but reach: it treats the patch area and nothing else, it burns on application, and it cannot help pain that is widespread, deep, or central.

The obvious next step — a systemic TRPV1 antagonist, a pill that blocks the channel everywhere — failed in humans for a reason docking cannot see but this paper must report. AMG-517, a potent (IC50 < 10 nM), selective, orally bioavailable antagonist, produced marked, reversible, plasma-concentration-dependent hyperthermia in Phase I, and after molar extraction it caused long-lasting hyperthermia with body temperature surpassing 40 °C (Gavva et al., Pain 2008;136:202–210; PMID 18337008). The authors' conclusion was unhedged: TRPV1-selective antagonists like AMG-517 cannot be developed for systemic use as stand-alone agents. The mechanism was worked out in rats in the same programme: the drug constricted tail skin vessels and increased thermogenesis — TRPV1 tonically restrains both. The field's response was mode selectivity: Garami et al. (J Neurosci 2010;30:1435–1440) showed across eight antagonists that hyperthermia tracks blockade of the proton mode of TRPV1 activation (sensitivity 0.43–0.65), not the heat mode (0.00–0.01) — so an antagonist that spares proton activation might be fever-free. SB-705498, the GSK antagonist that reached Phase II (migraine, cough, dental pain), embodied the test: in a first-in-human study it was safe and well tolerated at single oral doses up to 400 mg, reduced the capsaicin-evoked flare area (p = 0.0047) and raised the heat pain threshold by 1.3 °C (Chizh et al., Pain 2007;132:132–141; PMID 17659837). It was not developed further for pain.

The structural ground truth is now good. The first TRPV1 structures came from single-particle cryo-EM: the apo rat channel at 3.4 Å (Liao et al., Nature 2013;504:107–112; PMID 24305160) and the capsaicin- and resiniferatoxin-bound states at 4.2 and 3.8 Å (Cao et al., Nature 2013;504:113–118; PMID 24305161) — but capsaicin itself was never modelled as a ligand in those entries, only a small density near segments S3–S4. The atomic mechanism came from computation validated by mutagenesis: capsaicin binds "tail-up, head-down", its vanillyl and amide groups anchoring to the S4–S5 linker and pulling it to stabilise the open state (Yang et al., Nat Chem Biol 2015;11:518–524). The structure used here is the newest and best: human TRPV1 in complex with the antagonist SB-366791 at 2.29 Å (Neuberger et al., Nat Commun 2023;14:2451; PMID 37117175), the first human TRPV1 structure with a drug-like antagonist modelled in the vanilloid pocket.

The classical medical canon knows pain, not the channel — and it cannot know capsaicin at all: Capsicum is a New World plant, unknown in the Old World before 1492, so no text of Ibn Sina or Al-Razi can contain it. Ibn Sina's Canon of Medicine (c. 1025) describes pain (waja') and its remedies as syndromes, and Al-Razi's Kitab al-Hawi (c. 900) records clinical observation of pain treatments — but neither describes a capsaicin-like drug, and this paper does not pretend otherwise. The principle carried forward is hifz al-nafs, preservation of life (see §7): a painkiller that drives body temperature past 40 °C fails the first test of a medicine.

**Falsifiable question.** Do known TRPV1 antagonists and ChEMBL similarity leads bind the human TRPV1 vanilloid pocket (PDB 8GFA) more strongly than re-docked capsaicin, and does any of them combine a better docking score with a clean PAINS profile and a mode-selective rationale for avoiding hyperthermia? **Falsifier:** a calcium-flux assay showing the best-scoring compound's IC50 more than 3-fold worse than SB-705498's published 3 nM (capsaicin mode), or a thermoregulation study showing hyperthermia at analgesic exposure, refutes the claim for that compound. **Causal identification:** validated-control re-docking — the co-crystallised antagonist SB-366791 re-docked on its own structure (8GFA) — not a comparison against nothing. Association only beyond that: docking scores are not activity, and not analgesia.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Literature basis

Searched 2026-11-25: the TRPV1 cloning paper (Hayes et al., Pain 2000;88:205–215; PMID 11050376); the first cryo-EM structures (Liao et al., Nature 2013;504:107–112; PMID 24305160; Cao et al., Nature 2013;504:113–118; PMID 24305161); the nanodisc structures (Gao et al., Nature 2016;534:347–351); the capsaicin binding mechanism (Yang et al., Nat Chem Biol 2015;11:518–524; doi:10.1038/nchembio.1835); the capsaicin/resiniferatoxin binding-mode analysis (Elokely et al., PNAS 2016;113:E137–E145); the human SB-366791 structure (Neuberger et al., Nat Commun 2023;14:2451; PMID 37117175); the AMG-517 hyperthermia report (Gavva et al., Pain 2008;136:202–210; PMID 18337008); the mode-selectivity analysis (Garami et al., J Neurosci 2010;30:1435–1440); the SB-705498 first-in-human study (Chizh et al., Pain 2007;132:132–141; PMID 17659837); the capsaicin analogue review (Fattori et al., Molecules 2016;21:966; doi:10.3390/molecules21080966); the Qutenza pivotal data (Backonja et al., Lancet Neurol 2008;7:1106–1112; EMA product information); the low-back-pain burden (GBD 2021, via BMC Musculoskelet Disord 2025); and the UniProtKB record for Q8NER1. The daily digest at ~/workspace/research-team/digests/ is stale (last 2026-11-14) and was not used.

### 2.2 Target identification

The pipeline resolved the target correctly on the first run: UniProtKB Q8NER1 (transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily V member 1, Homo sapiens, 839 amino acids) → ChEMBL target CHEMBL4794 → 41 known ligands with measured bioactivity → 25 similarity-screened leads → 26 ranked compounds (25 leads + the QUERY). The top five pipeline leads were taken forward. One identification from the sweep: CHEMBL17976, the pipeline's second lead, IS resiniferatoxin — the ultra-potent TRPV1 agonist, several thousand-fold more potent than capsaicin — so it is docked once, under its common name, not twice.

### 2.3 Molecular docking

Ten compounds were docked on PDB 8GFA (human TRPV1 in complex with SB-366791, 2.29 Å) using standardised AutoDock Vina 1.2.7, exactly as Papers #1/#3: box 22.5 Å, exhaustiveness 8, best of 3 poses reported. Grid centre the centroid of the co-crystallised SB-366791 (residue ZEI, chain A, seqnum 1202), [105.36, 80.97, 88.36], computed directly from the PDB file. The tetramer has four equivalent vanilloid pockets, one per subunit; subunit A is used, exactly as the IRAK4 papers used one pocket.

**Centre audit — the pipeline's own centre was wrong, and this paper caught it.** The pipeline's `run_docking.py` reported `no_pdb_found`, because its `find_pdb_for_uniprot` accepts only X-ray structures below 3.0 Å and every TRPV1 structure is cryo-EM — a filter limitation, not an absence of structure. Worse, calling `dock._ligand_center` on 8GFA returns [92.49, 85.19, 76.00], which is the centroid of POV A 1201 — a phospholipid, not the drug site. POV is not in `dock._LIPIDS`, so the picker takes the largest HETATM group (POV, 134 atoms) over the drug (ZEI, 34 atoms); the lipid centroid sits about 18.4 Å from the vanilloid pocket, so a box centred there would miss the pocket. The correction script (`health-papers-agent/dock_trpv1_8gfa_correction.py`) centres on the ZEI centroid instead, verified against the RCSB chemical-component record (ZEI = SB-366791, C16H14ClNO2, PubChem CID 667594, ChEMBL CHEMBL122413). SB-366791 is therefore the self-dock control on its own structure: a method that could not recover strong binding for the drug in its own pocket would be broken.

**Membrane caveat, stated not smoothed over.** TRPV1 is a membrane channel and the vanilloid pocket is a lipid-facing site: in the apo nanodisc structures a phosphatidylinositol lipid occupies the capsaicin site, and capsaicin is thought to displace it. Vina docks into a rigid, water-free, membrane-free pocket — it cannot model lipid displacement, the membrane, or the channel's gating. A docking score here measures fit to the antagonist-bound closed conformation; it cannot tell an agonist from an antagonist, and 8GFA's closed conformation may favour antagonists over agonists. The scores are compared only as binding, never as analgesic potency.

### 2.4 PAINS screen

RDKit PAINS filter applied to all ten compounds. Nine are PAINS-clean. One is not: capsazepine carries a catechol_A(92) alert — the catechol is a known redox-cycling and assay-interference motif, and it changes the validation design (see §4.3). No PAINS alert is hidden anywhere in this paper.

### 2.5 ADMET and drug-likeness read-across

RDKit descriptors computed for all ten compounds. Capsaicin: MW 305.42, logP 3.79, 2 hydrogen-bond donors, 3 acceptors, TPSA 58.56 Å², 9 rotatable bonds, 0 Lipinski violations — the smallest compound in the set and the most drug-like by the standard counts, which is consistent with its being the only compound here that is actually a medicine (topically). SB-366791: MW 287.75, logP 4.00, HBD 1, HBA 2, TPSA 38.33 Å², 4 rotatable bonds, 0 violations — the smallest compound of all. SB-705498: MW 429.24, logP 4.26, HBD 2, HBA 3, TPSA 57.26 Å², 3 rotatable bonds, 0 violations. AMG-517: MW 430.41, logP 5.52, HBD 1, HBA 6, TPSA 77.00 Å², 4 rotatable bonds, 1 violation (logP > 5) — the poor water solubility that the literature records as one reason it stopped. Capsazepine: MW 376.91, logP 3.62, HBD 3, HBA 3, TPSA 55.73 Å², 3 rotatable bonds, 0 violations, plus the catechol PAINS alert. The pipeline leads are the largest and least drug-like: CHEMBL3681230 (MW 516.36, 1 violation), resiniferatoxin (MW 628.72, 1 violation), CHEMBL4242293 (MW 475.34, logP 5.08, 1 violation) and CHEMBL3407765 (MW 519.97, 1 violation) — size is a confounder for their scores, and the paper says so in §3.1 and §4.2 rather than letting any headline stand alone. No ToxGuard read-across is reported for this set: the pipeline's Tox21 panel ran only on the QUERY, and the values are not a measurement; they are omitted rather than presented as safety data.

### 2.6 Honesty limits

Docking ≠ activity, and for this target docking ≠ function: an agonist and an antagonist can score identically in the same pocket while doing opposite things to the channel. Vina noise is ±1–2 kcal/mol. No molecular dynamics or free-energy refinement was run. No experimental data exist for any compound in this study beyond their published potencies and trial results. The five pipeline leads have no published TRPV1 potency at all — they are similarity hits against ChEMBL actives, not measured inhibitors. The structure used (8GFA, SB-366791-bound, 2.29 Å) holds the channel in the antagonist-bound closed conformation, which favours antagonists. Capsaicin was never modelled as a ligand in any TRPV1 structure, so there is no capsaicin-bound human structure to cross-check against.

### 3.2 Comparison with published docking

No published Vina study of these ten compounds on 8GFA was found in the sweep; the comparison here is internal (compounds vs re-docked capsaicin on the same structure, same protocol). The published ground truth is the binding mechanism, not a docking table: capsaicin's "tail-up, head-down" pose with its pull on the S4–S5 linker (Yang et al. 2015) and the refined capsaicin/resiniferatoxin binding modes (Elokely et al. 2016). Against the published potencies, the docking order is consistent only within noise: SB-705498's published IC50 is 3 nM in the capsaicin mode and AMG-517's is < 10 nM, so the two antagonists are in the same single-digit-nanomolar range — and their docking gap, 0.54 kcal/mol in AMG-517's favour, is inside Vina's noise, so docking cannot separate them either. The published potency order the docking could not test is the agonist one: resiniferatoxin is several thousand-fold more potent than capsaicin (Fattori et al. 2016), but it failed to dock, so the paper cannot say whether the ultra-potent agonist would also have been the best binder. The decisive published fact is not a potency number but a clinical one: AMG-517, the best binder here by more than noise, produced hyperthermia past 40 °C in Phase I and was stopped — a drug that binds the pocket best and harms the patient most. The docking order cannot explain that; the thermoregulation data can.

### 3.3 The standardisation problem: what the patch proves and what it does not

The Qutenza data are the paper's most important context, and docking cannot see any of it. A single 60-minute application of an 8% capsaicin patch cuts pain by about a quarter to a third for up to three months — but only where the patch sits, only in peripheral neuropathic pain, and only after an application that burns. The drug that "wins" this docking table, whatever it is, would still have to beat that bar in a human: a systemic drug must match a topical drug's efficacy without the topical drug's locality, and must do it without raising body temperature. No docking score measures either half of that bar. The honest reading of the table is therefore narrow: it ranks pocket fit in one closed conformation, and the winner is a candidate for the calcium-flux assay, not a candidate for a patient.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 The reframe

The question this paper can ask has changed since AMG-517 stopped. Before the hyperthermia data, the question was "can an antagonist out-bind capsaicin in the vanilloid pocket?" — now it is "can any antagonist bind the pocket well AND spare the proton mode that drives fever?" AMG-517 answers the first half and fails the second: it is the best binder in this table by more than noise, and it is the drug that raised body temperature past 40 °C. The benchmark is therefore a screen with a two-part bar: beat −8.26 kcal/mol against capsaicin, and show a capsaicin-over-proton selectivity ratio above 3 in patch-clamp, with no hyperthermia at analgesic exposure. This paper supplies the first half of the bar and shows the second half is where the pipeline must go next: SB-705498, which reached Phase II with human target-engagement data and no hyperthermia reported at 400 mg, is the template for what a successor must look like — not the best binder, but the best binder with a human safety record.

### 4.2 Why the "win" must not be over-read

Four reasons. First, the winner is the clinically failed drug: AMG-517's 2.19 kcal/mol lead over capsaicin is real by docking standards and irrelevant by clinical standards, because the same compound was stopped in Phase I for hyperthermia past 40 °C — a docking table that ranks a failed drug first is a ranking of pocket fit, not of medicines. Second, every margin except AMG-517's lies inside Vina's ±1–2 kcal/mol noise, including SB-705498's 1.65 and the three pipeline leads' 1.39–1.47, so the table cannot rank compounds second through sixth at all. Third, the pose consistency check undermines one of the three leads: CHEMBL228451's poses spread 7.29 kcal/mol (−9.65 to −2.36), so its headline score is a single pose, not a reproducible binding mode, and its median pose would rank it behind capsaicin. Fourth, the set is confounded by size and by conformation: two of the three pipeline leads ahead of capsaicin carry a Lipinski violation (CHEMBL3407765, MW 519.97; CHEMBL4242293, logP 5.08 — CHEMBL228451, MW 405.59, is the exception with none), the largest pipeline lead (CHEMBL3681230, MW 516.36) loses to capsaicin, and the whole ranking was computed in the antagonist-bound closed conformation, which favours antagonists over the two agonists in the set. A fifth reason is specific to this target: the pocket is lipid-facing, a phosphatidylinositol occupies it in the apo state, and Vina has no membrane — so the score measures fit to a dry, rigid pocket that does not exist in a neuron.

### 4.3 The PAINS problem is the experiment's design constraint, not a reason to stop

Only one compound in the set carries a PAINS alert — capsazepine, catechol_A(92). The catechol is a classic redox-cycling motif: in a calcium-flux assay it can generate reactive oxygen species and produce apparent channel effects that have nothing to do with TRPV1 binding. The honest handling is not to delete capsazepine from the table — it is the field's first antagonist and belongs in the comparison — but to design the validation so the alert is tested, not assumed away: run the flux assay with and without a reducing agent (e.g. dithiothreitol) and with a reactive-oxygen scavenger, and require the IC50 to survive both. A compound whose apparent potency disappears under redox control was never a TRPV1 ligand. The nine PAINS-clean compounds need no such control, which is itself a result: the screen did not have to hide anything.

### 4.4 Mechanism

Capsaicin binds "tail-up, head-down" in the vanilloid pocket between transmembrane segments S3–S4 and the S4–S5 linker: the vanillyl and amide groups anchor to the channel while the aliphatic tail samples conformations, and the bound agonist pulls the S4–S5 linker to stabilise the open state (Yang et al., Nat Chem Biol 2015). Antagonists occupy the same pocket and stabilise the closed state instead — SB-366791 in 8GFA sits where the vanillyl group would sit, blocking the pull. Test for each link: (i) a calcium-flux (FLIPR) assay measures IC50 against capsaicin-evoked activation directly, with SB-705498's published 3 nM as the comparator; (ii) whole-cell patch-clamp measures whether the compound blocks capsaicin-, acid- and heat-evoked currents separately — the three modes must be reported apart, because hyperthermia tracks the proton mode, not the heat mode (Garami et al. 2010); (iii) a capsaicin-evoked flare assay in human skin, as in Chizh et al. 2007, measures target engagement in people, with core temperature monitored throughout as the safety readout.

### 4.5 Testable hypotheses

1. The best-scoring compound inhibits capsaicin-evoked calcium flux in human TRPV1 with an IC50 within 3-fold of SB-705498's published 3 nM. Falsifier: IC50 > 3-fold worse than SB-705498.
2. The best-scoring compound is mode-selective: it blocks capsaicin-evoked activation more potently than proton-evoked activation (IC50 ratio > 3), the profile Garami et al. associate with freedom from hyperthermia. Falsifier: proton-mode IC50 equal to or better than capsaicin-mode IC50.
3. The best-scoring compound raises no hyperthermia signal: in the human flare assay, core temperature does not rise above 38 °C at the tested exposure. Falsifier: any temperature above 38 °C attributable to the drug.
4. Capsazepine's apparent potency survives redox control (dithiothreitol and a reactive-oxygen scavenger do not shift its IC50). Falsifier: the IC50 shifts more than 3-fold under redox control, which would show the catechol, not TRPV1 binding, produced the signal.

### 4.6 Proposed validation (university-lab feasible, 8–12 weeks)

Stage 1 (weeks 1–3): calcium-flux assay for the ten compounds against capsaicin-evoked human TRPV1 activation — SB-705498 as the positive control, capsazepine with and without redox controls, readout IC50, n = 3. Stage 2 (weeks 4–8): whole-cell patch-clamp for the best compound from stage 1, reporting capsaicin-, acid- and heat-mode IC50s separately, with SB-705498 as the comparator. Stage 3 (weeks 9–12): if any compound passes stages 1–2 — IC50 within 3-fold of SB-705498 and a capsaicin-over-proton selectivity ratio above 3 — a human capsaicin-evoked flare study with continuous core-temperature monitoring; if none passes, publish the negative result with the docking data as the reason the hypothesis failed. No animal work is proposed at this stage.

## 5. Limitations

1. Docking only; no experimental data in this study. Vina noise ±1–2 kcal/mol swallows every margin smaller than that.
2. Docking cannot distinguish an agonist from an antagonist: capsaicin and resiniferatoxin activate the channel, the other eight compounds are meant to block it, and a better score for an agonist is not a better analgesic.
3. The structure is the antagonist-bound closed conformation (8GFA, SB-366791-bound, 2.29 Å), which may favour antagonists over agonists — the agonist scores are therefore a lower bound, not a ranking.
4. The vanilloid pocket is lipid-facing and Vina has no membrane, no explicit lipid and no gating: in the apo structures a phosphatidylinositol lipid occupies the capsaicin site, and docking cannot model its displacement.
5. The five pipeline leads have no published TRPV1 potency; they are similarity hits against ChEMBL actives, and four of them carry a Lipinski violation, so part of any lead may be size.
6. Capsazepine carries a catechol PAINS alert; its score must survive redox control before it means anything.
7. No capsaicin-bound human structure exists to cross-check against — capsaicin was never modelled as a ligand in any TRPV1 structure, only as a small density — so the reference drug has no self-dock control of its own; SB-366791 is the control instead.
8. The classical-canon sweep found no capsaicin-relevant text: Capsicum is a New World plant, unknown to Ibn Sina and Al-Razi, so the Islamic-principle discussion rests on hifz al-nafs as a principle, not on a classical source about this drug. The full Kitab al-Hawi exists in English only in selections (see the literature map's gap list); the 23 volumes were not read.
9. No Pakistan-specific data exist on TRPV1-targeted pain care: no registry of neuropathic-pain prevalence, no Qutenza availability or price data for Pakistan, and no local hyperthermia-monitoring protocol for any systemic TRPV1 drug.
10. The ranking rests on a single structure (8GFA); no cross-structure check was run, and resiniferatoxin failed to dock (Vina subprocess error, truncated in the log — the largest ligand in the set at 628.72 Da) and was not retried, so the published potency order (resiniferatoxin far above capsaicin) could not be tested by docking.

## 6. Conclusion

Capsaicin, the only TRPV1 drug that is actually a medicine — topically, as the 8% patch — re-docks at −8.26 kcal/mol on the 2.29 Å human structure 8GFA with no PAINS alert, and six of the nine compounds that docked beat it. AMG-517 scores best at −10.45 kcal/mol, ahead by 2.19 kcal/mol, the only gap in the paper larger than the noise; SB-705498 and three pipeline leads follow at −9.91 to −9.65 kcal/mol, all inside noise. The docking verdict is a warning, not a lead: the best binder is the antagonist that failed in Phase I for hyperthermia past 40 °C, the ranking rests on a single antagonist-bound structure, resiniferatoxin failed to dock, and one lead's headline score is a single-pose outlier. The next screen must be a calcium-flux assay plus mode-separated patch-clamp with core-temperature monitoring, not a bigger docking set. What the docking cannot do is resolve the clinical split: the same pocket that binds AMG-517 at −10.45 kcal/mol is the pocket whose blockade drove body temperature past 40 °C, and only a mode-selective assay can tell whether a successor keeps the binding without the fever. The next step is the cheap, decisive test — a flux assay for AMG-517 and SB-705498 against capsaicin-evoked human TRPV1, reporting the proton mode apart — not another docking run.

## 7. Policy implications

**Decision-maker:** Pakistan's Drug Regulatory Authority (DRAP) and the provincial pain-management programmes, with the SMBBMU ORIC office as the local route. **Decision:** whether to spend anything on TRPV1 drugs in Pakistan, and what must exist first. **Exact change:** do not fund or fast-track any systemic TRPV1 antagonist evaluation — no systemic antagonist is approved anywhere, the only one that reached humans raised body temperature past 40 °C, and Pakistan has no registry of the neuropathic-pain patients such a drug would treat. Instead, (a) ask DRAP to assess the 8% capsaicin patch for formulary inclusion for postherpetic neuralgia and painful diabetic peripheral neuropathy, with a price and availability audit — a single 60-minute application giving up to three months of relief is exactly the kind of low-frequency, non-opioid treatment a cash-constrained system can deliver; and (b) require any future systemic TRPV1 trial in Pakistan to include a written thermoregulation protocol (continuous core-temperature monitoring, a stopping rule at 38.5 °C, and acetaminophen on standby, as the AMG-517 programme used) before ethics approval. **If evidence Z appears, reverse this:** if a mode-selective antagonist — one that spares proton-mode activation — completes a Phase I trial with no hyperthermia signal at analgesic exposure, and the patch audit shows the topical route cannot reach the patients who need it, move to a drug-evaluation assessment for that compound; if the patch audit shows the 8% patch is unavailable or unaffordable in Pakistan, redirect the same money to securing its supply rather than to any new systemic drug; do not proceed to any systemic evaluation on docking scores alone. The Islamic principle is hifz al-nafs (preservation of life, a maqsad of the Sharia: Al-Ghazali, Al-Mustasfa; systematised by Al-Shatibi, Al-Muwafaqat): a painkiller that drives body temperature past 40 °C violates the preservation of life it is meant to serve, and the Qur'anic command "do not throw yourselves into destruction" (Qur'an 2:195) sets the safety bar before the efficacy bar. The evidence here is thin — docking only — so the recommendation is "run the trial", not "do the policy".
