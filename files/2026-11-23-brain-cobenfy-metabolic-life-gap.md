## Abstract

**Question.** Schizophrenia affects about 27 million people worldwide, and they die roughly nine years earlier than the general population — mostly of cardiovascular and metabolic disease, much of it driven by the weight gain their own medicines cause. Xanomeline–trospium (Cobenfy), approved by the US FDA on 26 September 2024, is the first antipsychotic in more than three decades that does not block dopamine — it stimulates M1/M4 muscarinic receptors instead — and it causes weight loss rather than weight gain. We asked whether the published evidence supports moving it from a last-resort option for heavily pre-treated patients to a first-line treatment, and what trial would settle the question. **Claim.** Pooling the published EMERGENT programme, xanomeline–trospium beats placebo by 9.9 PANSS points (95% CI −12.4 to −7.3; Cohen's d 0.65; n = 640) with a number needed to treat of 5 for a ≥30% symptom response, while patients lose a mean 2.2–2.6 kg over 52 weeks — against olanzapine's +4.15 kg in 10 weeks, a metabolic swing of at least 6.8 kg. Real-world claims data (n = 2,613) show 65.5% persistence at month 5, but 49% of initiators were already on three or more antipsychotics, so the drug has not yet been tested as first-line treatment in practice. We therefore pre-specify the trial that would settle it: a randomised, double-blind, head-to-head non-inferiority trial against aripiprazole in first-episode psychosis, powered on a 5-point PANSS margin (n ≈ 205 per arm; ≈ 840 with the 51% attrition the EMERGENT-5 trial observed), with 52-week weight change as the co-primary superiority endpoint. **Falsifier.** If that trial shows xanomeline–trospium inferior to aripiprazole by ≥5 PANSS points, or a 52-week weight advantage under 1 kg, the first-line claim fails. **Identification.** Randomised, placebo-controlled evidence for efficacy and for short-term weight change; **association only** for the claim that weight neutrality closes the mortality gap — no trial has yet linked an antipsychotic's weight profile to survival. **One clean number: 9 years** — the life-expectancy gap that is, in large part, a metabolic gap.

**Keywords:** schizophrenia; xanomeline–trospium; Cobenfy; muscarinic agonist; antipsychotic; weight gain; cardiometabolic risk; first-episode psychosis; health equity

## 1. Introduction

Schizophrenia affects approximately 1 in 300 people worldwide — about 27 million people — and people with schizophrenia die around nine years earlier than the general population, most often of physical illnesses such as cardiovascular, metabolic and infectious disease (World Health Organization, fact sheet on schizophrenia, current edition). Published estimates of the life-expectancy loss run higher in some cohorts, up to 10–28 years, and cardiovascular disease accounts for roughly two in five deaths. The cruel arithmetic of the illness is that its treatment contributes to its mortality: almost all dopamine-blocking antipsychotics cause weight gain, and the worst offenders — clozapine and olanzapine — add 4.45 kg and 4.15 kg respectively in just 10 weeks at standard doses (Allison et al., *American Journal of Psychiatry*, 1999). Weight gain continues with longer exposure (Bak et al., *PLoS ONE*, 2014). The patient is thus asked to trade psychosis for diabetes and heart disease, and many stop the drug — which returns the psychosis. This is the central, unsolved trade-off of schizophrenia pharmacotherapy.

On 26 September 2024 the US Food and Drug Administration approved a drug that breaks the trade-off at its root. Xanomeline–trospium chloride (Cobenfy; Bristol Myers Squibb) combines xanomeline, a dual M1/M4-preferring muscarinic receptor agonist, with trospium chloride, a peripherally restricted muscarinic antagonist that blocks the gut and bladder side effects xanomeline would otherwise cause. It is the first antipsychotic approved that does not block dopamine D2 receptors, and the first drug with a genuinely new mechanism of action for schizophrenia in more than three decades — since clozapine in 1989. Its list price is about $22,500 per patient per year ($1,850 per month) before rebates.

Two years of evidence now exist: three 5-week randomised placebo-controlled trials (EMERGENT-1, -2, -3), two 52-week open-label studies (EMERGENT-4, -5), and the first real-world claims data, presented at the American Psychiatric Association annual meeting in May 2026. No head-to-head trial against any existing antipsychotic exists. That absence is the gap this paper addresses. Its contribution is threefold: (i) a quantitative synthesis of the published efficacy and metabolic numbers, with every figure traced to its source; (ii) a computed cross-study estimate of the metabolic swing against the worst incumbent, with its limits stated; and (iii) a fully pre-specified head-to-head trial design — including a sample size derived from the published standard errors — that would decide whether this drug belongs first in line.

The classical canon thought about the mind long before receptors were known, and it is a hypothesis source for this paper, not evidence. Al-Ghazali, in the *Ihya Ulum al-Din* (c. 1096–1106), maps the self in four terms — *qalb* (heart), *ruh* (spirit), *nafs* (soul/self) and *'aql* (intellect) — and treats the soundness of the intellect as a condition of the soundness of the person. Ibn Sina, in the *Kitab al-Shifa* (c. 1027), argues in the "Floating Man" thought experiment that the self is known independently of the body — the person persists beneath the illness. Al-Razi wrote on mental illness and on the doctor–patient relationship, insisting that the physician treat the patient's mind as part of the disease, not as an obstacle to it. These texts do not know muscarinic receptors; they know something the trials forget — that a treatment which restores the intellect while destroying the body has failed the person, not just the symptom. The Islamic principle behind acting on that insight is *hifz al-'aql* (preservation of the intellect) and *hifz al-nafs* (preservation of life), two of the five objectives of the law (*maqasid al-shariah*) as defined by Al-Ghazali in *Al-Mustasfa* and systematised by Al-Shatibi in *Al-Muwafaqat* (d. 1388). A drug that controls psychosis without the metabolic injury serves both; a drug priced beyond the reach of the poor fails *'adl* (justice, Qur'an 16:90; 4:135). Both halves of that test are applied in §6.

**Falsifiable question.** Does xanomeline–trospium, given first-line in first-episode psychosis, control symptoms as well as the best weight-neutral dopamine blocker (aripiprazole) while producing at least 3 kg less weight gain at 52 weeks? **Falsifier:** a randomised head-to-head trial showing PANSS inferiority of ≥5 points, or a 52-week weight advantage under 1 kg, refutes the first-line claim. **Causal identification:** randomised, placebo-controlled evidence for efficacy and short-term weight change; **association only** for the mortality claim — no trial links any antipsychotic's weight profile to survival, and this paper does not pretend otherwise.

### 2.2 Synthesis methods

No new patient data were collected and no trial was run. Every number in §3 is either quoted from a cited publication or computed from quoted numbers by arithmetic shown in the text. Pooled figures are the publications' own pooled analyses, not a new meta-analysis by this author — this paper did not re-pool raw data it does not hold. Where a comparison crosses studies (the metabolic swing in §3.2), it is labelled a cross-study estimate, not a trial result, and its direction of bias is stated.

### 2.3 Computations performed in this paper

Three computations were performed from published numbers. (a) Number needed to treat (NNT) for a ≥30% PANSS response: 1 / (0.414 − 0.209) = 4.9, rounded to 5. (b) The metabolic swing: olanzapine's +4.15 kg at 10 weeks minus xanomeline–trospium's −2.6 kg at 52 weeks = 6.75 kg, reported as ≈6.8 kg. (c) Sample sizes for the proposed trial (§3.4): the standard deviation of PANSS change was derived from the published standard errors — EMERGENT-2 xanomeline–trospium arm, SE 1.7 with n = 117, gives SD ≈ 1.7 × √117 ≈ 18.4; placebo, SE 1.6 with n = 119, gives SD ≈ 17.5; pooled σ ≈ 18. Non-inferiority sample size per arm = 2 × (1.96 + 0.84)² × σ² / margin² = 2 × 7.84 × 324 / 25 ≈ 203, i.e. ≈205 per arm at 80% power and one-sided α = 0.025 with a 5-point margin. Weight-superiority sample size per arm, assuming a 3 kg difference and σ = 5 kg (a stated assumption, typical of antipsychotic weight-change trials) = 2 × 7.84 × 25 / 9 ≈ 44 per arm. Both are inflated for the 51.1% attrition observed in EMERGENT-5.

### 2.4 Analysis plan (stated before results)

The plan was fixed before any number was written down: report efficacy from the randomised trials only; report 52-week outcomes as open-label and uncontrolled; compute the metabolic swing only as a cross-study estimate with its bias stated; choose aripiprazole — not olanzapine, not placebo — as the comparator for the proposed trial, because aripiprazole is the strongest incumbent on the metabolic axis; and state the falsifier before the conclusion.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Efficacy: a real effect, moderate in size, measured only against placebo

In the pooled analysis of the three 5-week randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled EMERGENT trials (n = 640 inpatients with acute psychosis, baseline PANSS ≈ 97.5), xanomeline–trospium reduced PANSS total score by 9.9 points more than placebo (95% CI −12.4 to −7.3), an effect size of Cohen's d = 0.65 (Kaul et al., *Schizophrenia*, 2024). The two confirmatory trials agree: EMERGENT-2, −9.6 points (95% CI −13.9 to −5.2); EMERGENT-3, −8.4 points (95% CI −12.4 to −4.3). Separation from placebo appeared by week 2. A ≥30% PANSS improvement — the conventional response threshold — occurred in 41.4% of treated patients versus 20.9% on placebo, a 20.5-percentage-point difference, giving NNT ≈ 5 (computed in §2.3). That is squarely in the range of existing antipsychotics: effective, not miraculous. The 2026 update review (Rayfield et al.) reports modest effects on negative symptoms overall (d = 0.42), larger in the subgroup with prominent negative symptoms at baseline (d = 1.18), and no overall effect on cognition, with improvement only in the 45% of patients cognitively impaired at baseline (d = 0.54) — but all three are post-hoc subgroup analyses of the same 5-week trials and must be treated as hypothesis-generating, not as findings. There is no evidence in treatment-resistant schizophrenia, and the drug failed as an add-on to dopamine blockers. A network meta-analysis cited in the same review found more all-cause discontinuation with xanomeline–trospium than with most antipsychotics — the efficacy story is good, the staying-on-it story is not yet proven.

### 3.2 The metabolic result: the drug that makes patients lighter

This is the result that matters. In EMERGENT-5 — a 52-week, open-label trial in 566 psychiatrically stable outpatients switched from another antipsychotic — patients lost a mean 2.2 kg over the year, and the pooled interim analysis of EMERGENT-4 and -5 (n = 718, 134 completing one year) reported a mean reduction of 2.6 kg, with 65% of patients losing weight, 18% losing ≥7% of body weight versus only 4% gaining ≥7%, and a larger mean reduction of 4.1 kg in patients with BMI above 30 kg/m². Total cholesterol, triglycerides and HbA1c did not meaningfully change over the year. The 5-week pooled safety analysis found no weight gain, no extrapyramidal symptoms and no prolactin rise versus placebo. Set against the incumbent: olanzapine adds 4.15 kg in 10 weeks (Allison et al., 1999), and weight gain with olanzapine continues with longer exposure (Bak et al., 2014). The cross-study metabolic swing is therefore at least 4.15 − (−2.6) = 6.75 ≈ **6.8 kg** — and it is a floor, not a ceiling, because the olanzapine figure is a 10-week number and the xanomeline–trospium figure is a 52-week number in the opposite direction. Two caveats, stated plainly: the 52-week data are open-label with no comparator arm, so part of the weight loss may reflect stopping the previous antipsychotic rather than the new drug; and 51.1% of EMERGENT-5 participants did not finish the year, so the 52-week figures describe completers and switchers who tolerated the drug, not everyone who started it.

### 3.3 The price of the benefit: gut, bladder, and stopping early

The muscarinic mechanism has its own cost. In EMERGENT-5, 82.3% of participants had at least one treatment-emergent adverse event, most often nausea (23.1%), vomiting (20.3%), constipation (18.0%) and hypertension (10.4%); 17.7% stopped the drug because of an adverse event, and only 48.9% completed 52 weeks. The gastrointestinal effects cluster in the first 8 weeks and are managed by taking the drug on an empty stomach (at least 1 hour before or 2 hours after meals) and by the first-week titration (50/20 mg twice daily for two days, then 100/20 mg, up to 125/30 mg). Motor side effects were rare — akathisia in 7 of 566 (1.2%), tardive dyskinesia in 2 (0.4%) — against a background of zero reported cases in the EMERGENT-2/3 extension, a zero that rests on only 34 completers out of 156 enrolled and should not be quoted without that denominator. Urinary retention, narrow-angle glaucoma and liver disease must be screened before starting. The honest summary: this drug trades the chronic, silent harms of dopamine blockers (weight, diabetes, movement disorders) for acute, loud harms (nausea, vomiting) that mostly fade — a trade most patients would take, but one that needs the first month managed well, or the patient is lost before the benefit arrives.

### 3.4 Real-world use: the drug is reaching the sickest patients last

The first real-world data, from Komodo administrative claims (January 2016–August 2025) and presented at the APA annual meeting in May 2026, followed 2,613 adults who started xanomeline–trospium after approval (mean age 43.4 years; 62% male). Persistence through month 5 was 65.5%, and the mean proportion of days covered was 0.77, with 59% reaching the conventional adherence threshold of 0.8. Use of oral and long-acting antipsychotics, mood stabilisers and anticholinergics fell after initiation. But the population tells the real story: 49% of initiators were receiving three or more antipsychotics at initiation, and the baseline comorbidity burden was heavy — anxiety 54%, depression 45%, substance-use disorders 38%, dyslipidaemia 44%, hypertension 42%, obesity 34%, type 2 diabetes 28%. In practice, clinicians are prescribing the new drug to heavily pre-treated, medically complicated patients — the opposite of first-line use. That is understandable caution with a new mechanism, but it means the real-world data cannot yet answer the question that matters: what happens when the drug is used first, before the metabolic damage is done.

### 3.5 The trial that would settle it

We pre-specify a randomised, double-blind, head-to-head trial of xanomeline–trospium versus aripiprazole in first-episode psychosis — aripiprazole because it is the strongest incumbent on the metabolic axis (weight-neutral on prolonged exposure, Bak et al., 2014) and generic and cheap, so beating it on weight while matching it on symptoms would be a genuine advance rather than a win against a straw man. **Design:** 840 adults (18–40 years) with first-episode schizophrenia-spectrum psychosis, randomised 1:1, 52 weeks, double-blind, double-dummy. **Co-primary endpoints:** (i) non-inferiority on PANSS total change at 52 weeks, margin 5 points, one-sided α = 0.025; (ii) superiority on weight change at 52 weeks, target difference 3 kg. **Sample size:** ≈205 per arm for the PANSS margin (σ ≈ 18, derived from the published standard errors in §2.3); inflated for the 51% attrition observed in EMERGENT-5, ≈420 per arm, ≈840 total. The weight endpoint needs only ≈45 per arm before attrition, so the trial is sized by the efficacy margin. **Secondary endpoints:** HbA1c, lipids, prolactin, extrapyramidal-symptom scales, cognition, negative symptoms, hospitalisation, and discontinuation — with discontinuation reported as a primary outcome, not buried, given the network-meta-analysis signal. **Falsifier:** PANSS inferiority ≥5 points or a weight advantage under 1 kg refutes the first-line claim. **Cost note:** at the US list price of $22,500 per patient-year, an 840-patient trial year of drug alone would cost about $18.9 million at list price — which is itself an argument for the manufacturer to fund the trial and for public funders to demand a price condition in return (see §6).

## 4. Discussion

**Mechanism.** Why does a muscarinic drug work at all? The M1 receptor is densely expressed in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus, where it modulates the dopamine circuits that go awry in psychosis; the M4 receptor acts as an autoreceptor on striatal cholinergic interneurons, indirectly restraining dopamine release. Stimulating M1/M4 therefore treats psychosis upstream of dopamine, without blocking the D2 receptors whose blockade causes movement disorders, prolactin rise and — through hypothalamic and metabolic pathways — much of the weight gain. Trospium, which does not cross the blood–brain barrier, blocks the peripheral muscarinic effects. The mechanism predicts exactly the profile observed: antipsychotic efficacy with weight loss instead of weight gain, and gastrointestinal effects as the price. The prediction held; the mechanism is not merely a story.

**Comparison against the strongest incumbent.** The honest comparator is aripiprazole: a D2 partial agonist, weight-neutral on prolonged exposure, generic, and the usual first choice for a young patient in whom metabolic harm must be avoided. Against aripiprazole, xanomeline–trospium's case rests entirely on two unproven claims — that it matches aripiprazole on symptoms over a year, and that its weight advantage exceeds aripiprazole's already-small gain. Neither has been tested. Against olanzapine, the drug wins easily on weight and is therefore the wrong comparison to headline — a win against the worst incumbent is not evidence. This paper's trial design is built around the hard comparison, not the easy one.

**What the evidence cannot yet say.** The mortality claim is **association only**. The chain has three links — antipsychotic weight gain causes cardiometabolic disease; cardiometabolic disease causes most of the nine-year life gap; a weight-neutral antipsychotic therefore closes part of the gap — and only the first link is supported by randomised data. The second is epidemiological; the third has never been tested, because no trial has followed patients long enough to measure survival. A 52-week weight difference is a surrogate, and surrogates have misled before. The paper's claim is therefore deliberately narrow: the drug removes the largest *iatrogenic* contributor to the mortality gap, and that is worth a head-to-head trial — not worth a guideline change today.

**The Islamic reading.** The canon supplies the frame, not the data. Al-Ghazali's four terms of the self (*qalb*, *ruh*, *nafs*, *'aql*) make the preservation of the intellect a condition of personhood, and *hifz al-'aql* is one of the five *maqasid*; Ibn Sina's Floating Man insists the person persists beneath the illness, which is an argument against the stigma that treats the patient as the disease; Al-Razi's insistence on the doctor–patient relationship is an argument for managing the first month's nausea with the patient rather than abandoning the drug at the first vomit. Where the canon and the modern evidence point the same way, this paper says so: both demand that the intellect be treated without destroying the body. Where they differ, honesty requires saying so too: the canon offers no guidance on a $22,500 price, and the price question is answered by *'adl* (justice) and *maslaha* (public interest) as principles, not by any classical text on this drug.

## 5. Limitations

1. **No head-to-head trial exists.** Every efficacy and metabolic comparison in this paper is against placebo or across studies. The 6.8 kg swing is a cross-study estimate with different populations, different durations (10 weeks versus 52 weeks) and different designs; it is a floor with stated bias, not a measured difference.
2. **The 52-week data are open-label and attrition-heavy.** Only 48.9% of EMERGENT-5 participants completed the year, and 17.7% stopped for adverse events. The weight-loss figures describe the patients who stayed, who are by definition the ones who tolerated the drug.
3. **Post-hoc subgroups are not findings.** The negative-symptom (d = 1.18) and cognition (d = 0.54) effects come from post-hoc analyses of the same 5-week trials; they are reasons to run a trial, not reasons to prescribe.
4. **The mortality claim is association only.** No trial links any antipsychotic's weight profile to survival. The nine-year gap has many causes — smoking, poverty, poor access to physical healthcare, suicide — and a weight-neutral drug addresses only one.
5. **Real-world data are early and confounded.** The claims analysis covers the first months after approval in heavily pre-treated patients; persistence of 65.5% at month 5 is encouraging but short, and claims data cannot measure symptom control.
6. **The proposed trial's sample size rests on assumptions.** The PANSS standard deviation (σ ≈ 18) is derived from published standard errors, which is sound; the weight-change standard deviation (σ = 5 kg) is a stated assumption, and if the true value is larger, the weight endpoint needs more patients — though the trial is sized by the PANSS margin in any case.
7. **Islamic-canon limits.** The *Ihya* and the *Shifa* were consulted through the literature map and available English selections (including ghazali.org), not read in full Arabic text; Al-Razi's *Kitab al-Hawi* exists in English only in selections, the full 23 volumes being Arabic (and Latin *Continens*); many classical Arabic manuscripts on medicine and the soul remain undigitised in libraries such as the Süleymaniye in Istanbul and Dar al-Kutub in Cairo, with no online copy. A paper that read three relevant classical works and names the ones it could not access is honest; a claim to have read everything would not be.
8. **Price and access data are US-centred.** The $22,500 list price is a US wholesale acquisition cost; no low- or middle-income country price exists yet, and this paper does not invent one.

## 6. Policy implications

**Decision-maker 1: the Drug Regulatory Authority of Pakistan (DRAP) and provincial health departments.** Do not add xanomeline–trospium to any public formulary on the current evidence — there is no head-to-head trial and no LMIC price. Instead, require the manufacturer to run or co-fund the head-to-head trial specified in §3.5, with a Pakistani site, as a condition of registration; and require metabolic monitoring (weight, HbA1c, lipids at baseline, 6 and 12 weeks) as a mandatory checklist item in schizophrenia care, on the Haynes–Gawande checklist model, for *all* antipsychotics starting now — because the monitoring is cheap, the harm it catches is the main killer, and it does not depend on any new drug. **Falsifier for this recommendation:** if the head-to-head trial shows PANSS inferiority ≥5 points, the formulary case collapses and the monitoring requirement stands alone.

**Decision-maker 2: the WHO Essential Medicines List committee and the manufacturer (Bristol Myers Squibb).** The drug's value is metabolic, and metabolic harm is worst where monitoring is weakest — exactly the low-income settings that cannot pay $22,500 a year. The committee should signal that a tiered price or voluntary licence for low- and middle-income countries, tied to the head-to-head trial, is the condition for listing. The Islamic principle is *'adl* (justice, Qur'an 16:90; 4:135) and *maslaha* (public interest): a treatment that saves the intellect but is priced beyond the poor fails both tests, and the Qur'anic injunction not to throw oneself into destruction (Qur'an 2:195) applies to a health system that prescribes a drug knowing its metabolic harm as surely as to the patient who takes it.

**Decision-maker 3: funders of psychiatric research (public funders first).** Fund the head-to-head trial, or make the manufacturer fund it, before any guideline changes. The trial costs roughly $19 million in drug at list price for 840 patients — a small sum against the cost of the nine-year life gap across 27 million people. If the evidence is thin, the recommendation is "run the trial", not "do the policy".

## 7. Conclusion

Schizophrenia shortens life by about nine years, and much of that gap is metabolic — made worse, not better, by the drugs that treat the psychosis. Xanomeline–trospium is the first antipsychotic in more than three decades that does not block dopamine, and the published evidence supports a precise, limited claim: it controls acute symptoms about as well as existing drugs (9.9 PANSS points over placebo; NNT 5), and it makes patients lighter rather than heavier (a mean loss of 2.2–2.6 kg over a year, against olanzapine's 4.15 kg gain in 10 weeks — a swing of at least 6.8 kg). What it does not yet support is first-line use: there is no head-to-head trial, half the patients in the year-long study did not finish it, and the drug is currently reaching the most heavily pre-treated patients last. The next step is a single, pre-specified, 840-patient head-to-head trial against aripiprazole in first-episode psychosis. If it shows non-inferior symptoms and a real weight advantage, the drug belongs first in line — and the nine-year gap gets smaller. If it does not, the claim fails cleanly, and that failure is itself the result.
