## Abstract

**Question.** Pakistan has raised its statutory tax rates repeatedly for a quarter of a century — general sales tax from 16% to 18%, a new super tax up to 10%, a 29% corporate rate stacked with surcharges — yet its tax-to-GDP ratio has not durably risen. Is the country on the wrong side of the fiscal curve Ibn Khaldun described in 1377, where higher rates shrink the base faster than they raise the yield?

**Claim.** Yes — on the evidence available, Pakistan sits past the peak of its Khaldunian curve. Over 2001–2024 the tax-to-GDP ratio moved only inside a 7.9–11.4% band (mean 9.7%), and the Federal Board of Revenue's own ratio stayed in single digits for 24 consecutive fiscal years (2000-01 to 2023-24), while statutory rates rose at every margin. The binding constraint is the base, not the rate: about 5.9 million returns were filed for tax year 2025 in a country of 241.5 million people, and only about 3 million taxpayers show any taxable income at all.

**Falsifier.** The claim is refuted if either (i) the FBR-only tax-to-GDP ratio rises above 12% and holds there for three consecutive fiscal years with no new statutory rate increase, or (ii) a legislated cut in a major rate — general sales tax back to 16%, or super-tax relief — is followed within two fiscal years by a fall, rather than a rise, in the FBR tax-to-GDP ratio. The fiscal year 2025-26 outturn (target Rs 14.131 trillion) is the first scheduled test.

**Identification.** **Association only.** This is a descriptive time-series test on published aggregates, with no control group and no randomised or quasi-experimental variation in rates. Confounders — GDP growth, inflation, IMF programme conditions, enforcement drives — are named, not controlled. The paper falsifies the claim that "rate rises raise the ratio"; it does not estimate a causal effect of any single rate. A regression-discontinuity design on the February 2023 GST rise (17% → 18%) and a randomised rate pilot are proposed as the causal tests.

**One clean number: 24 years below 10%** — the number of consecutive fiscal years the FBR's tax-to-GDP ratio stayed in single digits while rates rose. The stakes number is **3 million**: the people in a country of 241.5 million who show any taxable income.

**Keywords:** Ibn Khaldun; Laffer curve; tax-to-GDP ratio; Pakistan; FBR; fiscal policy; tax base; super tax; maqasid; 'adl.

## 1. Introduction

Every Pakistani budget of the last decade has done the same thing: raised a rate. The general sales tax (GST) went from 16% to 17% in 2013 and to 18% in February 2023. A super tax on high earners was invented in 2022 at 1–4% and raised to 10% in 2023. The corporate rate stands at 29%, with banks at 42–43%, and the Overseas Investors Chamber of Commerce and Industry (OICCI) reports that the effective burden on large-scale industries approaches 49% once surcharges stack. And after all of it, the tax-to-GDP ratio — the single number that says whether a state can pay for itself — has barely moved. The Federal Board of Revenue (FBR) itself reports that its tax-to-GDP ratio stayed in single digits for 24 consecutive fiscal years, from 2000-01 to 2023-24. The World Bank lent $400 million in 2019 to lift the ratio to 17%; the target was later cut to 10%.

This is not a new puzzle. In 1377, Ibn Khaldun wrote in the *Muqaddimah* — in the Rosenthal translation, the passage every economics textbook now quotes — "It should be known that at the beginning of the dynasty, taxation yields a large revenue from small assessments. At the end of the dynasty, taxation yields a small revenue from large assessments." His mechanism was dynastic: as a dynasty ages, its cohesion (*'asabiyyah*) decays, its spending grows, and it imposes fresh taxes and raises old rates — "but the effects on business of this rise in taxation make themselves felt," enterprises contract, and revenue falls. Six centuries later Arthur Laffer drew the same curve on a napkin; the profession calls it the Laffer curve, and Laffer himself credited Ibn Khaldun as the originator. A 2018 study of Malaysia (Annuar et al., *ISRA International Journal of Islamic Finance*) tested the Khaldunian curve directly with an autoregressive distributed lag model on 1996–2014 data and found the inverted U-shape, with a revenue-maximising corporate rate of 25.5156%.

This paper applies the same test to Pakistan, the country where the question matters most urgently: a state that cannot fund its schools, clinics or courts from its own revenue, that returns to the IMF every few years, and that answers each crisis by raising rates on the same narrow base. The test is deliberately simple and deliberately honest about its limits. It asks whether 24 years of published data are consistent with the Khaldunian prediction — rates up, ratio flat, base narrow — and it states in advance what evidence would kill the claim.

The method standard is the one this programme practises: Banerjee and Duflo's demand for one small testable question; Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson's use of a source of variation no one can manipulate; Rodrik's growth diagnostics — find the binding constraint before prescribing; Deaton's insistence that measurement is the argument. The binding-constraint diagnosis is the paper's central move: if the constraint is the rate, raise it; if the constraint is the base, widen it. Twenty-four years of data say the constraint is the base.

### 2.2 Pre-registered analysis plan (stated before results)

1. **Assemble the rate history** from primary legislation and official notifications: GST 16% → 17% → 18%; super tax 0% → 4% → 10%; corporate rate and surcharges.
2. **Assemble the revenue series** on two definitions, kept strictly separate: (a) the FBR-only tax-to-GDP ratio (federal collections), and (b) the all-levels-of-government ratio (OECD/CEIC). Report both; never mix them.
3. **Test the Khaldunian prediction** in its weakest falsifiable form: if rates rose and the ratio did not durably rise, the "rate rises raise the ratio" claim fails; if the base stayed narrow while rates rose, the mechanism (base contraction) is consistent with the data.
4. **Stress-test against the incumbent's best year** (FY2024-25): if the strongest enforcement year on record broke the ceiling durably, say so and weaken the claim accordingly.
5. **Recommend policy only for the account that survives**, with a stated reversal rule and a named causal test for the future.

### 2.3 Identification (stated formally)

**Association only.** The design is a descriptive time series on national aggregates. There is no control group, no random assignment of tax rates, and no instrument. Rate changes coincide with IMF programmes, inflation shocks, floods, and enforcement drives, any of which could move the ratio independently. The paper therefore claims a *pattern* (rates up, ratio flat, base narrow) and a *falsification* (the rate-raises-revenue claim fails on its own terms), not a causal effect size for any rate. The causal tests that could identify one are specified in §4.3: a regression discontinuity around the February 2023 GST rise, and a randomised low-rate pilot on a defined taxpayer segment.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 The rates rose at every margin

- **General sales tax.** The standard rate was 16%, raised to 17% in the Finance Bill 2013-14 (a rise the Supreme Court later examined in *Engineer Iqbal Zafar Jhagra v Federation of Pakistan*, 2013), and raised again to 18% with effect from 14 February 2023 by SRO 179(I)/2023, confirmed in the Finance (Supplementary) Bill 2023 — a Rs 170 billion "mini-budget" and an explicit IMF programme condition. Luxury items were put at 25%.
- **Super tax.** Introduced by the Finance Act 2022 (Income Tax Ordinance 2001, section 4C) at graduated rates of 1–4% on income above Rs 150 million, with 10% for fifteen specified industries above Rs 300 million. The Finance Act 2023 raised the schedule to 1–10%, with 10% on income above Rs 500 million (8% on Rs 400–500 million, 6% on Rs 350–400 million); banking companies pay 10% above Rs 300 million. The levy was litigated immediately: the Sindh High Court declared the tax-year 2022 charge ultra vires, and the Lahore and Islamabad High Courts ruled against retrospective application of the higher rates.
- **Corporate and other rates.** The standard corporate rate is 29% (20% for small companies; 42% for banks in tax year 2027, down from 43%). Federal excise duty on cigarettes was raised alongside the GST rise, and FED on beverages and sugary drinks from 13% to 20%. Withholding taxes were extended year after year until they supplied 59% of all income-tax collection in FY2024-25.

### 3.2 The ratio did not move: 24 years below 10%

The FBR's own reporting, summarised in June 2025, is that its tax-to-GDP ratio remained in single digits in every fiscal year from 2000-01 to 2023-24 — 24 consecutive years. The anchor points: FY2021-22, 9.2%; FY2022-23, 8.5%; FY2023-24, 8.8% (Rs 9,299.1 billion collected against GDP of over Rs 105 trillion). The broader all-tax series (CEIC, from Ministry of Finance and Pakistan Bureau of Statistics data) tells the same story on a slightly higher level: 2001–2024, mean 9.7%, all-time high 11.4% in 2017, record low 7.9% in 2001, 10.3% in 2024. The OECD's all-levels-of-government measure puts Pakistan at 12.3% in 2024 (up from 10.6% in 2023 and 9.3% in 2011) — higher because it counts provincial taxes the FBR series excludes — against an Asia-Pacific average of 19.7% and an OECD average of 34.1%.

Three facts make the flatness striking rather than trivial. First, the *level* of collections rose enormously in nominal rupees (Rs 9.3 trillion to Rs 11.7 trillion in a single year, §3.4) — but the ratio is the test, and the ratio is what did not move. Second, the flat band survived every kind of government and every kind of reform: military and civilian, IMF programme and no programme. Third, the 2017 peak of 11.4% — the best year in the series — was followed by retreat, not by a new plateau; the ratio does not ratchet up, it reverts.

**Uncertainty on the ratio.** The two definitions differ by 1.5–3.5 percentage points in the same year (FBR-only 8.8–10.3% versus OECD all-levels 12.3% in 2024), because the FBR series excludes provincial taxes and the OECD series includes them. The paper's 24-year claim is anchored to the FBR-only series, which is the series the FBR itself reported as single-digit for 24 years; the OECD series is reported alongside so the reader can see the definitional gap. No conclusion in this paper depends on which definition is used: on either definition, the ratio is far below the 15% the World Bank and IMF treat as the minimum for sustainable growth, and far below regional peers (India 17%, Bangladesh 12%, per OICCI).

### 3.3 The base stayed narrow while the rates rose

The mechanism Ibn Khaldun named — rising rates shrink the base — is visible in the filer counts:

- **Income tax:** 4,738,595 active income-tax filers in FY2023-24 (FBR report). Against a 2023 census population of 241,499,431, that is about 2.0% of the population.
- **Sales tax:** only 234,193 individuals and entities filed sales-tax returns in FY2023-24 — roughly one sales-tax filer for every twenty income-tax filers, in an economy where GST supplies about a third of federal revenue.
- **The filer surge that bought nothing.** The Auditor General of Pakistan found returns filed rose 76%, from 2.959 million (tax year 2023) to 5.215 million (tax year 2024) — yet the tax-to-GDP ratio *fell* over the comparable window, from 10.6% in 2016-17 to 8.7%. The audit's explanation: most new filers entered the system to obtain reduced withholding rates on property and vehicle transactions, not to declare income. For tax year 2025, 5.9 million returns were filed (up 17.6%), but about one-third carried zero tax, and only about 3 million of 6.53 million active taxpayers show any taxable income at all — about 1.2% of the population.
- **The missing 20 million.** FBR's own estimates, cited in the *Business Recorder* analysis of the FBR Year Book, put the number of individuals liable to file at no more than 20 million. The gap between 20 million liable and ~5–7 million filing is the base that rate rises never reach.
- **Tax paid without filing.** 120 million unique mobile-phone users pay 15% advance income tax on their usage — roughly 25 times the number of active income-tax filers. The state already taxes a quarter of the population's phones; it cannot get a fortieth of the population to file a return. Withholding taxes supplied 59% of income-tax collection in FY2024-25: the system collects at source from people it never registers, and calls the result compliance.

### 3.4 The incumbent's best year: FY2024-25

Fiscal year 2024-25 is the strongest possible test of the rival account — that the ratio can be raised by squeezing harder at existing rates — because it was the strongest enforcement year on record. FBR collected Rs 11,744.3 billion, up 26.3% (Rs 2,445.2 billion) on FY2023-24's Rs 9,299.1 billion, the largest annual addition ever. The composition: income tax Rs 5.784 trillion (+28%), sales tax Rs 3.9 trillion (+26%), customs Rs 0.767 trillion (+16%), federal excise Rs 1.284 trillion (+27%).

The decomposition of the increase is the paper's most informative number. Of the Rs 2,445.2 billion increase, autonomous growth — what the economy would have delivered at unchanged policy — contributed only Rs 766 billion (31%), because inflation fell to 0.3% in the last quarter, GDP grew about 2.5%, and large-scale manufacturing contracted 1.52%. Policy measures contributed Rs 805 billion (33%) and enforcement Rs 865 billion (35%). The enforcement figure is 8.2 times the previous best on record (Rs 105 billion). Without policy and enforcement, collection would have been about Rs 10.07 trillion.

Three conclusions follow. First, the ratio did rise — to 10.3%, the first time the FBR series has crossed 10% in the 24-year window, and the strongest single-year improvement in 23 years by the FBR Chairman's own account. Second, it still missed the target: the original parliamentary target was Rs 12.97 trillion (shortfall about Rs 1.23 trillion); even the twice-revised target of Rs 11.9 trillion was missed by about Rs 0.17 trillion. Third, the increase was bought by squeezing the existing base harder, not by widening it: the filer counts above are from the same period, withholding remains 59% of income tax, and the World Bank's own verdict on its $400 million Pakistan Raises Revenue programme — whose target was a 17% ratio — was to downgrade the programme and cut the target to 10%. A system that needs record enforcement to reach 10.3% has not escaped the Khaldunian trap; it has demonstrated it. The scheduled test is FY2025-26, whose target is Rs 14.131 trillion: if the ratio holds above 12% for three years with no new rate rise, the falsifier in the Abstract is triggered and this paper's claim falls.

### 3.5 The cross-country benchmark: where the peak sits

The only peer-reviewed ARDL test of the Khaldunian curve in a Muslim-majority economy is Annuar et al. (2018) on Malaysia, 1996–2014: an inverted-U relationship between the corporate tax rate and corporate tax revenue, with a revenue-maximising rate of 25.5156% and long-run unidirectional causality from the rate to revenue. Pakistan's headline corporate rate of 29% already sits 3.5 points above that estimated optimum; with the super tax, a large company's headline rate reaches 39% — 13.5 points above it — and OICCI reports effective burdens approaching 49% for large-scale industries once surcharges stack. The Malaysian optimum is not Pakistan's optimum — different economy, different base, different period — and this paper does not transplant it. It is used as the paper's benchmark for what "past the peak" looks like in a comparable setting, and as the prior that the proposed Pakistani ARDL replication (§4.3) would test against.

The regional comparison points the same way. OICCI's *Pakistan's Tax Paradox* (reported by *Dawn*, 22 February 2026) describes Pakistan's system as "a narrow tax base and high tax rates, with tax revenues remaining largely stagnant as a share of GDP despite growth in absolute collections," and notes the ratio stuck at 9–10% for over a decade against India at 17% and Bangladesh at 12%. OICCI's own proposal — corporate tax phased from 29% to 25% over three years, super tax eliminated, GST from 18% to 17% and then 15% — is, in substance, the Khaldunian prescription, proposed by the taxpayers themselves. Where the taxed and the theorist agree, the disagreement is not about the direction; it is about whether the state will do it.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Mechanism: Khaldun's chain, in modern dress

Ibn Khaldun's mechanism has four links, and each has a modern Pakistani analogue, stated here as the hypothesis the data are consistent with — not as a proven causal chain:

1. **Cohesion decays, spending grows.** Khaldun: the dynasty's *'asabiyyah* weakens, rulers' "needs and exigencies grow… owing to the luxury in which they have been brought up." Modern analogue: a state that funds itself by borrowing and by IMF programmes rather than by a broad tax bargain has weak fiscal cohesion — it does not need to persuade citizens to pay, so it never builds the bargain.
2. **Fresh taxes and higher rates follow.** Khaldun: rulers "impose fresh taxes on their subjects… and sharply raise the rate of old taxes to increase their yield." Modern analogue: GST 17% → 18% by SRO in February 2023; super tax 4% → 10%; withholding extended to mobile-phone top-ups, bank transactions, property and vehicle transfers.
3. **Business contracts and the base shrinks.** Khaldun: "the effects on business of this rise in taxation make themselves felt… Cultural enterprises grow and increase [only] because the low taxes bring satisfaction." Modern analogue: activity migrates to untaxed or lightly taxed margins — agriculture, real estate, cash trade, the informal sector; firms litigate rather than comply (the super tax spent its first years in the Sindh, Lahore and Islamabad High Courts); individuals file nil returns for the transaction benefit rather than declaring income (Auditor General's finding, §3.3).
4. **Revenue falls — or, in the modern version, stalls.** Khaldun: "taxation yields a small revenue from large assessments." Modern analogue: the ratio stays in a 7.9–11.4% band for 24 years; each rate rise is followed by a revenue bump that does not survive as a higher ratio; the World Bank cuts its target from 17% to 10%.

The alternative mechanism — that the flat ratio reflects low state capacity rather than high rates — is not excluded by this paper and is partly true: a state that cannot register 20 million liable filers has a capacity problem. But capacity and rates interact in exactly the Khaldunian direction: high rates raise the return to evasion, which raises the cost of enforcement, which the state meets with more withholding at source — taxing phones instead of incomes. The FY2024-25 decomposition (§3.4) is the evidence: 35% of the increase came from enforcement, the most expensive and least durable margin.

### 4.2 Comparison against the strongest incumbent

The strongest incumbent explanation is the IMF/FBR model: raise statutory rates, widen withholding, and enforce harder on the existing base. It is the strongest because it is partly right — FY2024-25 proved that enforcement can move the ratio, from 8.8% to 10.3% in one year, the best single-year improvement in 23 years. It fails as a *durable* explanation on three counts. First, the improvement was bought with Rs 865 billion of enforcement, 8.2 times the previous record: a margin that cannot be repeated at that multiple every year. Second, the target was still missed — by Rs 1.23 trillion against the original target — so the model under-delivered on its own terms. Third, the model's own sponsors have retreated from it: the World Bank cut the programme target from 17% to 10%, and OICCI, the chamber of the largest taxpayers, now proposes the opposite instrument (lower rates, wider base).

The second incumbent, the "capacity-only" account — that Pakistan's ratio is low only because the state cannot administer taxes — fails differently: it cannot explain why the ratio was equally flat in the years when capacity was being built (the $400 million PRR programme, the Track and Trace system, IRIS 3.0) or why a 76% surge in filers coincided with a *falling* ratio. Capacity matters, but capacity spent on squeezing the same base is capacity spent on the wrong margin. The Khaldunian account survives both comparisons because it is the only one consistent with all four facts: rates up, ratio flat, base narrow, enforcement exhausted.

### 4.3 What this paper does not claim — and the causal test it proposes

This paper does not estimate the revenue-maximising tax rate for Pakistan, does not prove that any particular rate cut would raise revenue, and does not claim that Ibn Khaldun's dynastic theory is a complete theory of a modern fiscal state. It claims a pattern and a falsification, and it proposes the test that would settle the causal question:

1. **Regression discontinuity on the GST rise.** The February 2023 rise from 17% to 18% was imposed by SRO on a single date (14 February 2023) for administrative reasons tied to the IMF programme, not to the business cycle. Monthly GST collection and registered-filer counts around that date, with a discontinuity design, can estimate the causal effect of a one-point rise on revenue and on registrations.
2. **A randomised low-rate pilot.** Offer a randomly selected group of non-filing traders a reduced effective rate (for example, the Tajir Dost scheme at a lower presumptive rate) against a control group at the standard rate, and measure registration, declared income and revenue per trader over two years. This is the Banerjee–Duflo template applied to taxation: shrink the grand question into one small testable question, randomise, measure.
3. **An ARDL replication of Annuar et al. (2018) on Pakistani data.** The same inverted-U specification, on FBR corporate collections and the statutory corporate rate 1996–2025, would estimate Pakistan's own revenue-maximising rate — the number this paper deliberately does not invent.

## 5. Policy implications

**Decision-makers:** the Federal Minister for Finance and Revenue and the Chairman of the Federal Board of Revenue, acting through the Finance Act and FBR administration; the National Assembly's Standing Committee on Finance as the oversight body; the World Bank and IMF as the programme counterparts whose targets the policy must satisfy.

**Exact changes:**

1. **Freeze statutory rates for three fiscal years (FY2026-27 to FY2028-29).** No increase in GST, corporate rate, super tax or withholding rates; every new revenue measure must come from the base. Success metric: the FBR-only tax-to-GDP ratio, published monthly, with the base (active filers, nil-filers, withholding share) published alongside it.
2. **Legislate the OICCI glide path, conditional on base targets.** Corporate tax 29% → 25% over three years and GST 18% → 15%, each step conditional on the active-filer count rising by at least 1 million in the preceding year. The conditionality is the point: the rate cut is earned by base growth, so the policy cannot be attacked as a giveaway.
3. **Redirect the enforcement machine from squeezing to registering.** The Rs 865 billion enforcement capacity demonstrated in FY2024-25 should be retargeted at the ~13–15 million liable non-filers, starting with the 120 million mobile users already paying 15% advance income tax — a population the state already reaches, whose incomes it already touches, and whom it has never registered. Success metric: registrations from this population, published quarterly.
4. **Publish a monthly "base versus rate" dashboard.** Filers, nil-filers, withholding share of income tax, and the FBR-only ratio — so that the next rate rise, if anyone proposes one, must answer the dashboard first.
5. **Commission the causal tests in §4.3 now.** The GST regression discontinuity can be run on data the FBR already holds; the randomised pilot can be fielded in one city in one quarter. No rate cut should be made permanent before the pilot reports — and no rate rise should be made before the discontinuity study reports.

**Reversal rule.** If the FBR-only ratio rises above 12% and holds for three consecutive years with no new rate increase, the "stuck below the ceiling" claim is refuted and the freeze should be lifted. If a legislated rate cut is followed within two years by a fall in the ratio, the low-rates-raise-revenue half of the claim is refuted and the cut should be reversed. If neither happens, the policy continues.

**The Islamic principle behind the recommendation.** *'Adl* — justice: "O you who believe, stand out firmly for justice, as witnesses to God, even if it be against yourselves, your parents, and your relatives" (Qur'an 4:135); "Indeed, God commands justice and good conduct" (Qur'an 16:90). A tax system that takes 59% of its income tax by withholding at source, from people it never registers, while 15% is levied on the phone top-ups of 120 million users — many of them poor — and while large firms litigate the super tax in the High Courts, distributes its burden by convenience of collection rather than by justice. *Hifz al-mal* — preservation of wealth, one of the five objectives of the law (Al-Ghazali, *Al-Mustasfa*; systematised by Al-Shatibi, *Al-Muwafaqat*; extended to freedom and justice by Ibn Ashur, *Maqasid al-Shari'ah al-Islamiyyah*): a rate that destroys the enterprise it taxes destroys the wealth the law exists to preserve, which is exactly Ibn Khaldun's mechanism. *Maslaha* — public interest: the cost–benefit test must count the 20 million unregistered citizens and the unborn, not only next year's collection. *Shura* — consultation (Qur'an 42:38): a rate rise imposed by SRO without parliamentary approval — the practice the Supreme Court examined in 2013 and that recurred in February 2023 — is the opposite of consultation; Al-Mawardi's *Al-Ahkam al-Sultaniyya* makes lawful authority, not executive convenience, the condition of taxation. And the positive model is already in the tradition: *zakat* is a flat 2.5% levy on wealth above a threshold, owed by every Muslim who holds it (Qur'an 9:60 names its recipients) — a low rate on a broad base, designed for compliance rather than extraction. Where the Islamic sources and the modern evidence point the same way, this paper says so: both demand low rates on a wide base, justice in who bears the burden, and consultation before the rate is set. Where they differ, the paper would say so; here they do not.

## 6. Conclusion

For 24 years Pakistan raised its tax rates and its tax-to-GDP ratio did not durably rise: the FBR's ratio stayed in single digits from 2000-01 to 2023-24, the broader series averaged 9.7% with a 7.9–11.4% band, and the base stayed at about 2% of the population filing and about 1.2% showing any taxable income. The strongest enforcement year on record, FY2024-25, lifted the ratio to 10.3% — and still missed its target by over a trillion rupees, with two-thirds of the increase coming from policy and enforcement rather than from the economy. That is the pattern Ibn Khaldun described in 1377: "at the beginning of the dynasty, taxation yields a large revenue from small assessments. At the end of the dynasty, taxation yields a small revenue from large assessments." The falsifiable policy that follows is a three-year rate freeze, a conditional glide path down (corporate 29% → 25%, GST 18% → 15%), enforcement retargeted at the 13–15 million liable non-filers, a public base-versus-rate dashboard, and the causal tests that would settle the question properly. If the ratio breaks 12% and holds for three years without a new rate rise, or if a rate cut is followed by a falling ratio, this paper's claim falls and the policy reverses with it. That is the point: a fiscal claim that cannot be killed by the next three budgets is not a policy, it is a habit.

## 7. Limitations

1. **Association only.** There is no causal identification in this paper: no control group, no instrument, no discontinuity exploited. Rate changes coincide with IMF programmes, inflation, floods and enforcement drives. The claim is a pattern and a falsification, not an effect size.
2. **Two definitions of the ratio differ by 1.5–3.5 points.** The FBR-only series (8.8–10.3%) and the OECD all-levels series (12.3% in 2024) measure different things; the 24-year claim is anchored to the FBR-only series as the FBR reported it. A reader who prefers the OECD definition should read the claim as "below 13% for the available series" — the substantive conclusion is unchanged, but the headline number moves.
3. **Press-reported figures.** FBR collections, filer counts, the AGP audit findings and the OICCI report are used as reported in the press (June 2025–February 2026), because the underlying FBR Year Book and audit report were not directly accessed in this run. Any figure that the FBR's own publications revise should supersede the press figure; the paper's claims are stated so that they survive small revisions (the band, not any single year, carries the argument).
4. **The FY2024-25 jump is a genuine counter-signal.** The ratio crossed 10% for the first time in the window, driven by record enforcement. If FY2025-26 and FY2026-27 hold above 12% without new rates, the falsifier is triggered and the claim is refuted; this paper's own test is already running.
5. **The Malaysian optimum does not transfer.** Annuar et al.'s 25.5156% is Malaysia's revenue-maximising corporate rate for 1996–2014, estimated with controls this paper does not replicate. It is a benchmark and a prior, not Pakistan's number; the ARDL replication proposed in §4.3 is the work that would estimate Pakistan's.
6. **Post-window legislation.** The Finance Act 2026 (June 2026) reportedly changed super-tax thresholds and rates after the analysis window closed; those changes are not in the data and are not evaluated here. They are, however, a live natural experiment on the paper's own question, and the FY2025-26 outturn should be read against them.
