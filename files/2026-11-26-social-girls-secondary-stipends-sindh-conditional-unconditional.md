## Abstract

**Falsifiable claim.** In Sindh, Pakistan, a stipend for girls' secondary schooling that is *conditional* on 70% attendance raises girls' secondary enrolment by at least 6 percentage points over three years relative to no transfer, and beats an *unconditional* transfer of equal value by at least 3 percentage points. **Falsifier:** if the pre-registered cluster-randomised trial returns a conditional-vs-control effect of 0 percentage points or less, or a conditional-minus-unconditional gap below 3 percentage points, the claim that conditionality adds value in Sindh is rejected and the same budget should be paid unconditionally.

**Why this question, why now.** Pakistan has 25.37 million children aged 5–16 out of school, including 13.41 million girls; Sindh alone accounts for 7.82 million out-of-school children, 4.09 million of them girls (Girls Education Statistics and Trends Report 2023–24). Enrolment collapses after primary school — 20.82 million children enrol at primary level but only 9.21 million reach middle school — and in rural Sindh the net enrolment rate for girls at secondary level is 6%. Two rigorous experiments speak directly to the design choice but disagree on what matters: Punjab's Female School Stipend Program, a conditional transfer evaluated by difference-in-differences and regression discontinuity, raised girls' middle-school enrolment by about 9% in relative terms (Chaudhury and Parajuli, 2008, 2010), while a randomised trial in Malawi found the unconditional arm achieved only 43% of the conditional arm's dropout reduction — yet unconditional transfers reduced teenage pregnancy and marriage far more (Baird, McIntosh and Özler, 2011). No experiment has tested conditionality head-to-head in Pakistan. This paper (i) re-uses the published Punjab evaluation to derive a cost-effectiveness benchmark — approximately US$263 per additional girl kept in school for a year (2007 prices) — (ii) scales it transparently to Sindh, and (iii) pre-registers a three-arm cluster-randomised trial (conditional vs unconditional vs control, 240 schools) that settles the question for Sindh's own context. The policy recommendation is conditional on the trial result, not ahead of it.

**Keywords:** conditional cash transfer; girls' education; Sindh; Pakistan; cluster-randomised trial; difference-in-differences; cost-effectiveness; maqasid al-shariah

## 1. Introduction

Pakistan has the world's second-largest population of out-of-school children (UNICEF). The Girls Education Statistics and Trends Report 2023–24 puts the number at 25.37 million children aged 5–16 out of school — 13.41 million girls and 11.96 million boys. Punjab carries the largest absolute burden (9.6 million), but Sindh's crisis is structurally worse: 7.82 million out-of-school children, including 4.09 million girls, in a province where 44% of school-age children are out of school. The problem in Sindh is not mainly failure to start school; it is failure to continue. Nationally, 20.82 million children enrol at primary level, but the number falls to 9.21 million at middle school, 4.70 million at matriculation and 2.83 million at intermediate level. In Sindh the pipeline narrows to a bottleneck: the province has roughly 36,000 primary schools but only 2,634 middle schools and 1,674 secondary schools, and about 54% of children exit education after completing primary school.

The gender gradient is steepest where the pipeline is narrowest. In Sindh, girls' net enrolment at primary level reached only 49% in 2019–20 (up from 46% in 2007–08 — three percentage points in twelve years). At secondary level, the net enrolment rate for girls in rural Sindh is 6%, against 14% at elementary level; only 19% of rural women in Sindh are literate, against 64% in urban areas. Girls make up 54% of Sindh's out-of-school children, and 60% at middle level.

Governments already pay families to keep girls in school. The question is no longer *whether* to pay, but *whether to condition the payment on attendance* — and the two best pieces of evidence point in different directions on different outcomes. Punjab's Female School Stipend Program (FSSP), launched in 2004 in the 15 of Punjab's 34 districts with the lowest literacy, paid PKR 600 per quarter to girls in grades 6–8 in government schools conditional on 80% attendance (extended in 2006 to high school at PKR 900 per quarter). Evaluated with school-census data for 2003–2005 using difference-in-differences, triple differences and regression discontinuity, the programme raised female enrolment in grades 6–8 by about 9% in relative terms — roughly six additional girls per school — with estimates ranging from 9 to 23 percentage points across specifications (Chaudhury and Parajuli, 2008, 2010). Five years on, beneficiary girls were 3–6 percentage points more likely to complete middle school and 4–6 percentage points more likely to transition to high school (Baez, Alam and Del Carpio, 2011).

The only experiment that randomises conditionality itself, however, complicates the story. Baird, McIntosh and Özler (2011, *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 126: 1709–1753) randomised 2,907 adolescent girls aged 13–22 across 176 enumeration areas in Zomba district, Malawi, to a conditional transfer (US$1–5 per month to the girl plus US$4–10 to the parent, conditional on 80% attendance), an unconditional transfer of identical value, or control. The unconditional arm's dropout reduction was only 43% as large as the conditional arm's, and the conditional arm outperformed on English reading comprehension. But teenage pregnancy and marriage were substantially *lower* in the unconditional arm — entirely because of effects among girls who had dropped out of school, whom the conditional arm by design stopped paying. Conditionality buys schooling; unconditionality protects the girls schooling fails to reach.

Sindh has never run this test. The federal Benazir Taleemi Wazaif programme — a conditional cash transfer for children of Benazir Income Support Programme (BISP) Kafalat beneficiaries, piloted in five districts in November 2012 and expanded to all districts by July 2020, now covering over 12 million children — is conditional nationwide (70% quarterly attendance; PKR 3,000 per quarter for a secondary-school girl, PKR 4,000 at higher secondary, plus a one-time PKR 3,000 bonus for girls completing primary). It has never been evaluated against an unconditional arm of equal value. This paper's contribution is a design that does so, with the analysis plan stated before any data are collected, and a cost-effectiveness benchmark computed transparently from the published Punjab evaluation so that the trial's result can be judged against a known price.

## 2. Literature and the Islamic canon

### 2.1 Modern evidence

The causal-education literature gives this paper its method standard. Banerjee and Duflo's randomised-evaluation programme (J-PAL; Nobel Prize in Economics 2019) established that a small, testable question answered by randomisation beats a grand reform asserted without one. Heckman's Perry Preschool follow-up showed a 6–10% annual return per dollar invested in early human capital — the fiscal argument for investing in children rather than the sentimental one. Hanushek's value-added work insists on measuring the output (learning) rather than the input (spending); this paper therefore pre-specifies enrolment *and* learning outcomes, not disbursement counts. Chetty's Opportunity Atlas shows that place — down to the neighbourhood — causally shapes a child's adult outcomes; Sindh's district-level variation in girls' enrolment is the same phenomenon at provincial scale.

Within cash transfers specifically, the evidence base is: Chaudhury and Parajuli (2008, 2010) on Punjab's FSSP (difference-in-differences, triple difference and regression discontinuity on the 2003 and 2005 school censuses); Baez, Alam and Del Carpio (2011) on the FSSP's longer-term effects on completion, transition, marriage and labour-force participation; Baird, McIntosh and Özler (2011) on the Malawi conditionality experiment; and the BISP's own programme documentation for Benazir Taleemi Wazaif (pilot 2012, national coverage 2020, 70% attendance condition, published stipend rates). The gap this paper fills: no head-to-head conditional-vs-unconditional test exists in Pakistan, and no published cost-per-additional-girl figure exists for the Punjab programme. Both are supplied here — the first as a design, the second as a computation.

### 2.2 The Islamic canon on education

The Islamic tradition treats the education of girls not as a modern development goal but as a religious duty. The first revealed word of the Qur'an is a command to read: "Read, in the name of your Lord who created" (Qur'an 96:1–5). The Qur'an asks, "Are those who know equal to those who do not know?" (Qur'an 39:9). The Prophet ﷺ is reported to have said, "Seeking knowledge is a duty upon every Muslim" (Sunan Ibn Majah 224; scholars differ on the grading of this narration — al-Albani graded it sahih, others da'if — and the paper relies on the Qur'anic command, which is not in dispute, for the normative claim). Classical scholarship systematised the point: Al-Ghazali, in *Al-Mustasfa*, defined the five objectives of the law (maqasid al-shariah), including preservation of intellect (*hifz al-'aql*); Al-Shatibi systematised the maqasid in *Al-Muwafaqat* (d. 1388); Muhammad al-Tahir Ibn Ashur expanded them to freedom, justice and equality in *Maqasid al-Shari'ah al-Islamiyyah* (written 1946). Ibn Khaldun devoted chapter 6 of the *Muqaddimah* (1377) to education as a craft acquired by habituation — a claim this paper's mechanism section tests directly: attendance is the habit, and the stipend is the incentive that builds it. Where the Islamic sources and the modern evidence point the same way — that keeping girls in school is among the highest-return uses of public money — this paper says so; where they differ, it says so too: the tradition's duty is unconditional (knowledge is owed to every Muslim, girl or boy, in school or out), while the *instrument* tested here is conditional. That tension is exactly what the unconditional arm of the trial measures.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Analysis plan (stated before results)

This paper contains no new field data. It contains (a) a re-analysis of published estimates into a cost-effectiveness benchmark, with every arithmetic step shown, and (b) a pre-registered trial design. The distinction is stated here so that no reader mistakes a computation for a finding: **the cost-effectiveness figures in Section 4.1 are extrapolations from published estimates, not new causal estimates — association and arithmetic only, in bold.** The causal claim is the trial's to settle, and its analysis plan is fixed below before any school is randomised.

### 3.2 The cost-effectiveness computation

Inputs (all published, all cited in Section 1): FSSP stipend spending in 2007, US$7.3 million; beneficiaries in 2007, 245,000 girls in grades 6–8; preferred treatment-effect estimate, +6 female students per school (difference-in-differences combined with regression discontinuity, Chaudhury and Parajuli); middle schools covered by the programme, 4,621. Computation: 4,621 schools × 6 additional girls = 27,726 additional girls enrolled. Marginality: 27,726 ÷ 245,000 beneficiaries = 11.3% — roughly one in nine stipend recipients was an additional girl in school; the other eight would have enrolled anyway. Cost per additional girl-year: US$7,300,000 ÷ 27,726 ≈ **US$263**. Sensitivity: if the true effect were +3 girls per school, US$527; if +9, US$176. All figures are 2007 US dollars, not inflation-adjusted — the limitation is stated, not hidden.

### 3.3 The trial design (pre-registered, to be run)

**Design.** Three-arm cluster-randomised controlled trial in Sindh, randomised at school level, stratified by district and by baseline girls' enrolment. Arms: (A) conditional stipend — PKR 3,000 per quarter per secondary-school girl (the Benazir Taleemi Wazaif secondary rate), conditional on 70% quarterly attendance; (B) unconditional transfer — PKR 3,000 per quarter per secondary-school girl, no attendance condition, paid to the same household on the same schedule; (C) control — existing Taleemi Wazaif only, no additional payment. **Population:** government girls' middle and secondary schools in Sindh's lowest-enrolment districts (Thatta, where 71% of children are out of school, and the other bottom-quintile districts on PSLM 2019–20). **Duration:** three school years. **Primary outcome:** girls' net enrolment rate at secondary level, measured by independent school-census audit (not programme records). **Secondary outcomes:** attendance rate, grade progression, standardised Urdu and mathematics scores (Hanushek's output test), dropout, and — following Baird et al. — marriage and pregnancy among girls who leave school. **Identification:** randomisation at school level; intention-to-treat estimation; difference-in-differences on PSLM waves as a secondary check. **Power:** to detect a 6-percentage-point effect on enrolment from a 50% baseline at 5% significance with 80% power requires about 1,089 girls per arm if individually randomised; with clustering (intra-cluster correlation 0.05, 40 girls per school, design effect 2.95) this becomes about 3,212 girls — about 80 schools — per arm, or 240 schools in total. All power assumptions are stated and can be re-run. **Pre-registration:** design, outcomes and the falsifier in the Abstract are fixed before randomisation; any post-hoc analysis will be labelled exploratory.

### 3.4 Mechanism

The causal chain, with a test for each link: (1) cash relaxes the household budget constraint — tested by whether enrolment rises in both paid arms; (2) the attendance condition changes the *marginal* decision to send a girl on a given day — tested by the CCT-minus-UCT gap in attendance; (3) attendance becomes habit, in Ibn Khaldun's sense of education as a craft acquired by repetition — tested by whether the enrolment effect persists in the year after payments stop; (4) schooling delays marriage — tested by the marriage outcome in each arm, with the Malawi result predicting the unconditional arm does better here. If link (2) fails but link (1) holds, conditionality is pure administrative cost and the policy should be unconditional. If link (4) reverses the ranking on welfare even while schooling favours the conditional arm, the recommendation must weigh the two outcomes explicitly rather than declare a winner on enrolment alone.

### 3.5 Comparison against the strongest incumbent

The incumbent is not "nothing". It is Benazir Taleemi Wazaif as it operates today in Sindh: conditional, PKR 3,000 per quarter for a secondary-school girl, 70% attendance, delivered through BISP to Kafalat households. The trial's control arm keeps that programme. The question is therefore narrow and honest: does *adding* conditionality discipline, or *removing* it, beat the status quo per rupee? A design that compared a stipend against nothing would flatter any transfer; this one does not.

### 4.2 Scaling to Sindh (back-of-the-envelope, assumptions stated)

Sindh has 4.09 million out-of-school girls. Suppose the policy goal is 100,000 *additional* secondary-school girls in Sindh — about 2.4% of the out-of-school stock. At the Punjab marginality rate (11.3%), reaching them requires paying about 885,000 girls (100,000 ÷ 0.113). At the Taleemi Wazaif secondary rate for girls (PKR 3,000 per quarter = PKR 12,000 per year), the annual stipend bill is about **PKR 10.6 billion**, or **PKR 106,000 per additional girl kept in secondary school for a year**. That is the paper's single memorable number — and it is a warning as much as a price: nine-tenths of a universal stipend budget pays girls who would have enrolled anyway. Targeting (poorer households, lowest-enrolment districts, girls at the dropout margin) is where the cost-effectiveness lives, and the trial's stratification is designed to find it.

### 4.3 What the trial must show — and what would kill the claim

The claim survives only if the conditional arm beats control by at least 6 percentage points on secondary net enrolment *and* beats the unconditional arm by at least 3 points. It dies if either condition fails. A second, independent falsifier already exists in the literature and must be respected: if Sindh's rollout reproduces the Malawi pattern — conditionality winning on schooling while losing on marriage and pregnancy among dropouts — then a conditional-only scale-up is the wrong recommendation even if the enrolment test passes, and the policy must be a hybrid (conditional stipend in school, unconditional support for girls who leave).

## 5. Discussion

Three points follow. First, the Punjab evidence justifies paying for girls' schooling, but it does not justify paying *conditionally* — that was never the tested margin in Pakistan, and the one experiment that did test it found the answer depends on which outcome you care about. Second, the cost arithmetic disciplines enthusiasm: PKR 106,000 per additional girl-year is affordable at small scale and ruinous as an untargeted entitlement, which is why the trial stratifies by district enrolment and why the paper recommends starting in the lowest-enrolment districts rather than province-wide. Third, the supply side binds: Sindh has 2,634 middle schools against 36,000 primary schools, so a demand-side stipend in a district with no middle school buys nothing — the trial excludes schools beyond reasonable travel distance and the policy recommendation pairs stipends with the classroom construction the Civil Services Academy review identifies as the binding constraint.

The Islamic frame strengthens rather than softens the conclusion. Preservation of intellect (*hifz al-'aql*) makes girls' education a duty of the community, and justice (*'adl*, Qur'an 4:135) forbids a design whose burden falls on the poorest — which is precisely what an unconditional payment to the poorest households does better than a conditional one that stops paying the moment a girl drops out. Public interest (*maslaha*) requires counting the welfare of the girl who leaves school, not only the girl who stays. The trial is therefore not a technical nicety: it is the mechanism by which the state discovers whether its instrument serves the principle.

## 6. Limitations

1. **No new Sindh microdata were analysed in this run.** The Punjab difference-in-differences result is a published finding re-used here, not re-estimated; PSLM district microdata were not re-analysed. The trial is a design, not results.
2. **The 11.3% marginality rate is extrapolated across province and twenty years.** Punjab in 2004 is not Sindh in 2026: different baseline enrolment, different transfer size, different delivery system. The PKR 10.6 billion figure is a planning bound, not a budget.
3. **2007 US dollars are not adjusted for inflation or converted to 2026 rupees.** The US$263 and PKR 106,000 figures sit in different price years and must not be compared directly.
4. **Supply constraints may dominate.** Where no middle school exists within travel distance, no stipend design enrols anyone; the trial's exclusion of such areas limits generalisability to exactly the districts that need help most.
5. **External validity of the Malawi experiment is limited.** Different country, different transfer size (US$1–5 per month), different marriage norms. It motivates the unconditional arm; it does not predict its size in Sindh.
6. **Classical Islamic works were consulted through the verified catalogue in the project's literature map** (Ibn Khaldun, *Muqaddimah*, Rosenthal translation, via archive.org; Al-Ghazali selections via ghazali.org; Al-Shatibi and Ibn Ashur via the map's verified holdings), **not by a fresh full-text reading in this run.** The Qur'anic verses (96:1–5; 39:9; 4:135) and Sunan Ibn Majah 224 were verified against live sources in this run; the grading of Ibn Majah 224 is disputed among scholars and is reported as such. Untranslated and undigitised manuscripts — including most of Al-Razi's *Kitab al-Hawi* and the full *Kitab al-Tasrif* — were not accessible and are named here as the gap.
7. **The most recent daily research digest available to this run was 14 November 2026**; the digest series appears to have stopped after that date, so the literature sweep for this paper relied on targeted searches of the primary sources instead.

## 7. Policy implications

**Decision-maker:** Government of Sindh, School Education & Literacy Department, jointly with the Benazir Income Support Programme. **Exact change, in sequence:** (1) fund the three-arm cluster-randomised trial in 240 schools in Sindh's lowest-enrolment districts (80 schools per arm, three school years, independent census audit), at the Taleemi Wazaif secondary rate of PKR 3,000 per quarter; (2) if the conditional arm beats control by ≥6 percentage points *and* the unconditional arm by ≥3 points, adopt a conditional secondary-girls stipend in those districts, paired with middle-school construction where the school census shows no school within travel distance; (3) if it does not, pay the same PKR 3,000 per quarter unconditionally — the Malawi evidence says the unconditional arm protects out-of-school girls from early marriage and pregnancy, which the conditional arm cannot do by design; (4) in either case, publish the trial data as a public tool, in the manner of Chetty's Opportunity Atlas, so that districts can be targeted by evidence rather than by patronage. **Islamic principle:** *hifz al-'aql* (preservation of intellect, Al-Ghazali, *Al-Mustasfa*; Al-Shatibi, *Al-Muwafaqat*) and *'adl* (Qur'an 4:135) — the state owes every girl the means to learn, and a design that abandons the girl who drops out fails the duty even when it raises the average. **Falsifier for the policy:** if the trial returns a conditional-vs-control effect of 0 or below, the conditional stipend is not scaled, and the budget is reallocated to unconditional transfers or to school construction, whichever the trial shows works.

## 8. Conclusion

Sindh's 4.09 million out-of-school girls are the sharpest edge of Pakistan's education crisis, and the province's pipeline — 36,000 primary schools narrowing to 2,634 middle schools — guarantees the crisis reproduces itself every year. Punjab's conditional stipend worked, at about US$263 per additional girl-year in 2007 prices, but it never tested whether the condition was doing the work. Malawi's experiment says conditionality buys schooling and unconditionality protects the girls schooling misses. The honest next step is not to scale either answer province-wide, but to run the three-arm trial in Sindh that distinguishes them — and to let its pre-registered falsifier, not habit or ideology, decide which rupee is spent how. **One number to remember: PKR 106,000 — the cost of keeping one additional Sindh girl in secondary school for a year at Punjab's marginality rate, and the price of getting the design wrong.**
