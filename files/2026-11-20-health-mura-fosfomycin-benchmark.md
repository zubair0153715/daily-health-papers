## Abstract

Fosfomycin is the only clinically used inhibitor of MurA, the first enzyme of bacterial peptidoglycan synthesis, and it works by a covalent bond to the active-site cysteine Cys115 — a mechanism no non-covalent docking score can price. We asked whether ChEMBL-recorded MurA inhibitors and published non-covalent leads genuinely out-bind fosfomycin, or whether the comparison itself is broken. Using the LeadForge pipeline (UniProtKB P0A749 → ChEMBL target CHEMBL1984 → PubChem/RDKit, PAINS and ADMET screen → AutoDock Vina 1.2.7 on the 1.7 Å Escherichia coli MurA structure 3KQJ, grid centred on the co-crystallised UDP-N-acetylglucosamine), we re-docked fosfomycin and eight candidates on identical settings. Fosfomycin's non-covalent score is only −5.04 kcal/mol, yet its measured ChEMBL IC50 is 100 nM; seven of eight candidates out-score it, the best by 5.83 kcal/mol (CHEMBL5219133, −10.87 kcal/mol), and the most potent measured non-covalent inhibitor, ampelopsin (480 nM), scores −8.65 kcal/mol. The ranking is inverted against measured potency (Spearman ρ = −0.37, n = 6): the two best dockers are the weakest measured inhibitors (4.5–4.8 µM), and both carry logP 6.5–7.0 with two Lipinski violations. The honest limit is that a Vina score cannot rank a covalent drug against non-covalent candidates, the margins exceed Vina's ±1–2 kcal/mol noise only because the benchmark is meaningless, and the two flavonoid leads carry PAINS alerts. The claim that any candidate beats fosfomycin would be refuted if a MurA enzyme assay shows an IC50 above 50 µM, or if the candidate fails against the fosfomycin-resistant C115D mutant. The falsifiable next step is an 8–12-week enzyme-plus-MIC test of the two best dockers and ampelopsin against wild-type and C115D MurA.

## 1. Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance killed an estimated 1.27 million people directly in 2019, with a further 4.95 million deaths associated with resistant infection, and *Escherichia coli* was the leading pathogen in that burden. Uropathogenic *E. coli* (UPEC) causes more than 75% of urinary tract infections, the commonest bacterial infection of primary care, and fosfomycin — a 1969 natural product, a phosphoenolpyruvate analogue — is one of the few oral options left for multidrug-resistant UPEC. Its target, MurA (UDP-N-acetylglucosamine enolpyruvyl transferase, EC 2.5.1.7), catalyses the first committed step of peptidoglycan synthesis, the transfer of the enolpyruvyl moiety of phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) to UDP-N-acetylglucosamine. MurA has no mammalian counterpart, which makes it a clean antibacterial target in principle — and fosfomycin is still the only drug in the clinic that hits it.

Fosfomycin's potency is covalent. Its epoxide is opened by nucleophilic attack from the thiol of Cys115 in the MurA active site, forming a stable thioether that irreversibly inactivates the enzyme. The residue is not a passive anchor: replacing Cys115 with aspartate leaves a highly active enzyme that is fully resistant to time-dependent inactivation by fosfomycin, and the same Cys→Asp substitution is how mycobacteria and chlamydiae are intrinsically fosfomycin-resistant. Structural work then showed Cys115 is essential for product release rather than for the chemistry of enolpyruvyl transfer itself. Resistance in the clinic, however, most often bypasses the target altogether: mutations in the GlpT and UhpT transporters that carry fosfomycin into the cell, and plasmid-borne fosfomycin-inactivating enzymes — FosA (a glutathione S-transferase), of which FosA3 is the most widespread. In a 2024 study of 337 clinical UPEC isolates, 77% formed fosfomycin non-susceptible inner colonies during susceptibility testing, and loss of *uhpT* imposed no fitness cost in a murine urinary-tract model; a 2026 cohort of 158 UPEC isolates found 9.5% fosfomycin resistance, with *fosA3* present in a third of the resistant strains. A non-covalent MurA inhibitor that does not need Cys115, GlpT or UhpT would sidestep all three routes at once — which is why the field keeps searching for one, and why every new candidate is routinely "benchmarked" against fosfomycin by docking.

That benchmark is the problem this paper attacks. AutoDock Vina scores non-covalent complementarity only. It has no term for the covalent bond that supplies most of fosfomycin's potency, so fosfomycin's docking score measures only its weak reversible pre-complex — and any candidate that "beats" that score has beaten a straw man. The published literature already hints at the trap: a 2026 MurA docking study reported fosfomycin's non-covalent component at −4.5 kcal/mol while its optimised non-covalent ligands scored −7.3 to −7.7 kcal/mol, and presented that gap as progress. It is not evidence of progress; it is evidence that the ruler is wrong.

The classical medical canon knew the disease class long before the target: Ibn Sina's *Canon of Medicine* (c. 1025), Book II, catalogues plant materia medica for urinary and febrile illness, and Al-Razi's *Kitab al-Hawi* (c. 900) records clinical observation of infections. These texts are hypothesis sources, not evidence — they knew the syndrome, not the enzyme — but the two plant flavonoids tested here (ampelopsin, quercetin) sit directly in that tradition, and the Islamic principle behind acting on resistance is *hifz al-nafs*, preservation of life (see §7).

**Falsifiable question.** Do ChEMBL-recorded MurA inhibitors and published non-covalent leads bind the true MurA active site (PDB 3KQJ) at scores beyond re-docked fosfomycin, and does that binding track measured potency? **Falsifier:** a MurA enzyme assay IC50 above 50 µM for a candidate, or loss of activity against the fosfomycin-resistant C115D mutant for a supposedly non-covalent inhibitor, refutes the claim for that compound. **Causal identification:** validated-control re-docking — the reference drug re-docked on a co-crystal structure of its own target — not a comparison against nothing. Association only beyond that: docking scores are not activity, and for a covalent reference they are not even a ranking.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Literature basis

Searched 2026-11-20: the fosfomycin–MurA mechanism and Cys115 literature (structure, C115D mutant, product-release role), the ChEMBL record for *E. coli* MurA, recent non-covalent MurA inhibitor reports (flavonoids, pyrazolopyrimidines, machine-learning leads, repurposed drugs), and current fosfomycin-resistance surveillance. The daily research digest of 2026-11-14 was checked and carried no MurA-specific item; the topic rests on the targeted searches above. The Islamic-canon sweep covered the available digitised canon relevant to the question: Ibn Sina, *Al-Qanun fi al-Tibb* (c. 1025), Book II; Al-Razi, *Kitab al-Hawi* (c. 900) — consulted in the literature-map summaries, not in full Arabic text (see Limitations).

**An EBI-side outage, caught and corrected.** The LeadForge pipeline's ChEMBL bioactivity retrieval returned **0 records** for target CHEMBL1984 and therefore ranked only fosfomycin itself as a "lead". That zero is an EBI-side outage, not a wrong target and not a pipeline bug: the ChEMBL activity endpoint returned HTTP 500 for this target on 2026-11-20 (verified twice the same day, including by a direct check that returned HTTP 500 for the activity endpoint while the target endpoint itself returned HTTP 200), while direct activity queries made during the audit, in windows when the endpoint answered, show the target holds **522 bioactivities, including 119 nM IC50 records**. The outage is reported here, not hidden: all ChEMBL activity values in this paper come from direct ChEMBL API queries that returned successfully during the audit, and the supplementary docking script that uses them is preserved with the run. The most potent ChEMBL record for MurA is fosfomycin itself (CHEMBL1757, IC50 100 nM, assay CHEMBL2339337); the most potent non-fosfomycin record is CHEMBL2335230 at 2.8 µM. Older IC50 records in µg/mL from a single early assay (CHEMBL859884) were not used for ranking, because mass units without molar conversion cannot be ranked against nM records.

### 2.2 Molecular docking

Standardised AutoDock Vina 1.2.7 protocol, exactly as Papers #1/#3. Target: *E. coli* MurA, UniProtKB P0A749 (UDP-N-acetylglucosamine 1-carboxyvinyltransferase, 419 aa, gene *murA*); ChEMBL target CHEMBL1984 (*E. coli* K-12, single protein, component P0A749). Structure: PDB 3KQJ, the *E. coli* MurA binary complex with UDP-N-acetylglucosamine, 1.7 Å. Grid centre: centroid of the co-crystallised UDP-N-acetylglucosamine (HETATM UD1, chain A), [60.69, −44.29, 115.28], verified by direct computation with `dock._ligand_center` on the cached PDB file — the computed centre [60.693, −44.292, 115.277] matches the pipeline centre exactly, and the picker correctly ignored the isoaspartate residue, glycerol and phosphate in the file. Box 22.5 Å, exhaustiveness 8, best of 3 poses reported. The same centre, box and exhaustiveness were used for fosfomycin and all eight candidates. The structure is the closed, substrate-bound conformation: the active site is formed, which is the correct state for docking PEP-site binders. The classic fosfomycin co-crystal, 1UAE (1.8 Å, Skarzynski et al. 1996), was not used because 3KQJ is higher resolution and carries the same active-site geometry with the natural substrate bound.

**Compounds docked.** Fosfomycin (PubChem CID 446987, C3H7O4P, MW 138.06) plus eight candidates: the four most potent distinct ChEMBL MurA actives by nM IC50 — CHEMBL2335230 (2.8 µM), CHEMBL5219390 (4.5 µM), CHEMBL2335229 (4.6 µM), CHEMBL5219133 (4.8 µM) — and four literature inhibitors with published activity: ampelopsin (dihydromyricetin, PubChem CID 161557; MurA IC50 480 nM, the most potent of 13 flavonoids in Frlan et al. 2023), quercetin (PubChem CID 5280343; active flavonoid MurA inhibitor, same study), albendazole (PubChem CID 2082) and diflunisal (PubChem CID 3059), both with MIC 0.0625 mg/mL against *E. coli* (Zhang et al. 2022). SMILES for all eight were taken from the ChEMBL molecule records or PubChem and are preserved in the supplementary docking JSON. No compound was docked from an unverified structure.

### 2.3 PAINS screen

RDKit PAINS filter applied to all nine compounds. Fosfomycin and six candidates are clean. Ampelopsin and quercetin carry a PAINS alert (catechol moiety) — reported, never hidden; it changes the validation design (see §4.3). Note that a clean PAINS result does not exclude covalent reactivity: CHEMBL2335230 is a brominated nitro-furan and CHEMBL2335229 is bromonitromethane, both electrophilic chemotypes whose measured activity may involve covalent chemistry that Vina cannot model.

### 2.4 Honesty limits

Docking ≠ activity. Vina noise is ±1–2 kcal/mol. No molecular dynamics or free-energy refinement was run. No experimental data exist for these eight compounds against MurA in this study beyond the ChEMBL and literature records cited. Measured IC50 values below are ChEMBL or literature records against MurA, not new measurements. **Most importantly, Vina cannot price the covalent bond that makes fosfomycin work: fosfomycin's −5.04 kcal/mol is a score for its reversible pre-complex only, and comparing any non-covalent score against it as if it were a binding affinity is a category error.** The comparison is reported because the field performs it, and the point of this paper is that it is meaningless.

Seven of eight candidates out-score re-docked fosfomycin, by 1.28 to 5.83 kcal/mol, with tight pose consistency (top-3 spread ≤0.6 kcal/mol for the top two, ≤1.3 kcal/mol for all but one). The two best dockers are large, lipophilic thiazole–furan carboxylates (MW 509–563, logP 6.45–7.00, two Lipinski violations each). The most potent measured non-covalent inhibitor, ampelopsin (480 nM), docks fourth at −8.65 kcal/mol. The only candidate that scores worse than fosfomycin is bromonitromethane (−3.79 kcal/mol) — a 140 Da reactive fragment whose measured 4.6 µM activity is almost certainly covalent chemistry, not non-covalent binding, and which Vina accordingly cannot see. That single row is the control that proves the rule: where the mechanism is covalent, Vina under-scores; where it is non-covalent, Vina over-rewards size and lipophilicity.

### 3.2 The docking–activity discordance that must not be hidden

For the six compounds with a measured enzyme IC50 (fosfomycin, ampelopsin, and the four ChEMBL actives), the Spearman rank correlation between measured potency and docking score is **ρ = −0.37** — negative. The ranking is inverted: the most potent measured compound, fosfomycin (100 nM), docks fifth of six; the weakest measured compound, CHEMBL5219133 (4.8 µM), docks best; the second-weakest, CHEMBL5219390 (4.5 µM), docks second-best. Rigid Vina scoring on this pocket cannot rank these compounds, and it actively mis-ranks them in the wrong direction, because the score rewards molecular size and hydrophobic contact area (the two best dockers are the two largest and most lipophilic molecules in the set) while the one drug that works derives its potency from a single covalent bond to Cys115 that contributes nothing to the score. This is the paper's most important result, and it is a negative one: **a docking "win" against fosfomycin is not evidence of anything.**

### 3.3 Comparison with published docking

An independent 2026 MurA docking study (Kurniawan et al.) re-docked fosfomycin with an RMSD of 0.394 Å and reported its non-covalent component at −4.5 kcal/mol, against −7.3 to −7.7 kcal/mol for their optimised baeckenone derivatives. Our re-docked fosfomycin score of −5.04 kcal/mol agrees with that independent value within 0.5 kcal/mol — inside Vina noise — which validates our protocol and, more importantly, shows the field's published "improvements over fosfomycin" are the same artefact reproduced twice. No published Vina study of the four ChEMBL compounds or of ampelopsin/quercetin on 3KQJ was found in the sweep; the comparison here is internal (candidates vs re-docked reference on the same structure, same protocol). The re-docked fosfomycin score is the validated control that anchors the comparison — and what it validates is that the anchor itself is broken for this target.

### 3.4 The resistance problem the candidates must actually solve

A non-covalent MurA inhibitor is only useful if it escapes the three resistance routes that already limit fosfomycin. Transport: 77% of 337 clinical UPEC isolates form fosfomycin non-susceptible inner colonies, driven by *uhpT*/*glpT* loss that carries no fitness cost in the urinary tract. Enzymatic inactivation: *fosA3* is the most widespread plasmid-borne determinant, present in a third of fosfomycin-resistant isolates in a 2026 UPEC cohort. Target mutation: C115D MurA is fully active and completely fosfomycin-resistant, and it already exists in nature (mycobacteria, chlamydiae). None of the eight candidates has published data against any of these three routes. The precedent that a non-covalent inhibitor can clear the third route exists: the pyrrolidinedione series of Fathalla et al. (2022) inhibits wild-type and C115D MurA equally (most potent compound 46, IC50 4.5 µM) and is additive with fosfomycin. That is the standard a candidate must meet, and it is an enzyme-assay standard, not a docking standard.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 The reframe

The question this paper can actually answer is narrower than the one it started with. It cannot say "these compounds beat fosfomycin" — seven of eight out-score a benchmark that does not measure what fosfomycin does, the ranking is inverted against measured potency, and the two best dockers share the lipophilicity problem that kills most antibacterials before they reach the clinic. What it can say is: (a) the ChEMBL MurA record is thin — outside fosfomycin itself, the best measured inhibitor is 2.8 µM, and the pipeline run that day returned nothing only because the ChEMBL activity endpoint was returning HTTP 500; (b) the strongest measured non-covalent inhibitor remains the plant flavonoid ampelopsin at 480 nM, which docks well (−8.65 kcal/mol) but carries a PAINS alert; (c) the decisive experiment is now sharply defined — a MurA enzyme assay against wild-type and C115D enzyme, with fosfomycin as the comparator — and it is cheap and feasible in a university lab.

### 4.2 Why the "win" must not be over-read

Four reasons. First, the benchmark is a category error: Vina scores non-covalent complementarity, fosfomycin's potency is covalent, so the 5.83 kcal/mol "margin" of the best docker measures the size of the error, not the size of an advantage. Second, the ranking is inverted (ρ = −0.37): the best dockers are the weakest measured inhibitors. Third, the two best dockers are highly lipophilic (logP 6.5–7.0, two Lipinski violations, MW >500) — the same developability wall that every MurA programme has hit, since Gram-negative penetration punishes exactly this profile. Fourth, the single-structure, rigid-receptor protocol under-samples a target that works by induced fit: MurA's Pro112–Pro121 loop moves through multiple conformations on substrate binding, and one closed structure cannot represent the binding landscape.

### 4.3 Mechanism

MurA transfers the enolpyruvyl moiety of PEP to the 3′-hydroxyl of UDP-N-acetylglucosamine by an addition–elimination mechanism, with UNAG binding first and inducing the domain closure that forms the active site in the interdomain cleft. Cys115, on the flexible Pro112–Pro121 loop, acts as the general acid in protonation of C-3 of PEP and is essential for product release. Fosfomycin, a PEP analogue, enters the same pocket and is alkylated by Cys115. A non-covalent inhibitor must therefore do one of two things: occupy the PEP pocket tightly enough to compete with PEP without the covalent anchor, or bind the UNAG-induced closed conformation and prevent the loop movement that product release requires. Test for each link: (i) a MurA enzyme assay measuring enolpyruvyl transfer (phosphate release or coupled assay) against wild-type enzyme; (ii) the same assay against C115D MurA — a true non-covalent inhibitor must retain activity, a covalent one will not; (iii) MIC determination against *E. coli* with and without efflux-pump inhibition, to separate target inhibition from penetration failure — the exact failure mode Frlan et al. reported for most flavonoids, whose enzyme activity did not translate into antibacterial activity.

### 4.4 The PAINS problem is the experiment's design constraint, not a reason to stop

Ampelopsin and quercetin both carry a catechol PAINS alert, and Frlan et al. already showed their MurA inhibition is time-dependent and DTT-sensitive — the classic signature of redox-cycling or covalent artefacts from catechols. That does not make ampelopsin worthless (it is still the most potent measured non-covalent MurA inhibitor at 480 nM); it makes the assay design non-negotiable: every flavonoid measurement must be run with and without DTT, with and without pre-incubation, and with a detergent control against aggregation. If the 480 nM activity survives DTT, it is real; if it collapses, the compound is an artefact and the field should stop citing it. The same logic applies to the two electrophilic ChEMBL compounds: CHEMBL2335230 and CHEMBL2335229 must be tested for time-dependent inhibition and for activity against C115D before any claim of non-covalent inhibition is made.

### 4.5 Testable hypotheses

1. CHEMBL5219133 and CHEMBL5219390 inhibit wild-type *E. coli* MurA with IC50 ≤ 50 µM in a phosphate-release enzyme assay. Falsifier: IC50 > 50 µM for either compound.
2. At least one of the two retains activity (within 3-fold) against C115D MurA, proving a non-covalent mechanism. Falsifier: >10-fold loss against C115D.
3. Ampelopsin's 480 nM MurA inhibition survives 1 mM DTT in the enzyme assay. Falsifier: IC50 rises >10-fold with DTT, indicating a redox artefact.
4. Re-docking with molecular-dynamics refinement preserves the two leads' advantage over the flavonoids within 1 kcal/mol. Falsifier: the advantage disappears after minimisation.

### 4.6 Proposed validation (university-lab feasible, 8–12 weeks)

Stage 1 (weeks 1–3): recombinant *E. coli* MurA (wild-type and C115D) enzyme assay — phosphate-release readout, n = 3 — for CHEMBL5219133, CHEMBL5219390, ampelopsin and quercetin, with fosfomycin as the comparator and the pyrrolidinedione compound 46 as a positive non-covalent control; all flavonoid runs with and without DTT. Stage 2 (weeks 4–8): MIC determination against *E. coli* ATCC 25922 and a small panel of clinical UPEC isolates (including a *fosA3*-positive and a *uhpT*-mutant strain), with fosfomycin as comparator. Stage 3 (weeks 9–12): if any candidate passes stages 1–2, solubility and HepG2 cytotoxicity measurement (the published non-covalent leads L16 and X14 both cleared HepG2 at >100 µM, which is the bar); if none passes, publish the negative result with the docking data as the reason the hypothesis failed — the negative result is the paper's contribution, not a failure to report.

## 5. Limitations

1. Docking only; no experimental data in this study. Vina noise ±1–2 kcal/mol, and the fosfomycin benchmark is a category error (covalent drug scored by a non-covalent method).
2. Docking order is inverted against measured potency (Spearman ρ = −0.37, n = 6); rigid docking cannot rank these compounds, and the inversion is the finding, not a flaw to smooth over.
3. The two best dockers are highly lipophilic (logP 6.5–7.0, two Lipinski violations, MW >500); no solubility, penetration or protein-binding data were measured, and Gram-negative penetration punishes exactly this profile.
4. A single structure (3KQJ) was used; MurA works by induced fit and one closed structure under-samples the binding landscape.
5. Measured IC50 values are ChEMBL and literature records; assay conditions vary across source studies (fosfomycin's own recorded IC50s span assay-dependent values) and were not re-verified compound by compound.
6. The two flavonoid leads carry PAINS alerts and their published inhibition is DTT-sensitive; the two electrophilic ChEMBL compounds may act covalently. Neither mechanism was tested here.
7. No data against the three clinical resistance routes (*uhpT*/*glpT* transport loss, FosA enzymes, C115D MurA) were generated for any candidate.
8. The Islamic-canon sweep used the literature-map summaries of Ibn Sina's *Canon* and Al-Razi's *Kitab al-Hawi*; the full Arabic texts were not read, and other classical works on urinary and febrile illness were not individually consulted.

## 6. Conclusion

Seven of eight candidates out-dock fosfomycin on *E. coli* MurA (3KQJ) by up to 5.83 kcal/mol — and the result means nothing, because fosfomycin's potency is a covalent bond to Cys115 that Vina cannot price, its non-covalent score of −5.04 kcal/mol corresponds to a measured IC50 of 100 nM, and the docking ranking is inverted against measured potency (ρ = −0.37). The honest finding is narrower and more useful: the best measured non-covalent MurA inhibitor remains ampelopsin (480 nM, PAINS-flagged), the ChEMBL record outside fosfomycin tops out at 2.8 µM, and the two best dockers are lipophilic thiazole–furan carboxylates with no measured advantage. The next step is not another docking run — it is the cheap, decisive test: a MurA enzyme assay against wild-type and C115D enzyme, with DTT controls for the flavonoids. That test, not a docking score, is what would make a fosfomycin alternative real.

## 7. Policy implications

**Decision-maker:** Pakistan's Higher Education Commission research-funding panels and the SMBBMU ORIC office, with DRAP as the eventual regulator. **Decision:** whether to fund a small, targeted validation of existing ChEMBL and literature MurA inhibitors as an academic drug-discovery exercise. **Exact change:** commission the 8–12-week wild-type-plus-C115D enzyme screen proposed in §4.6 for CHEMBL5219133, CHEMBL5219390 and ampelopsin against fosfomycin, instead of funding another purely computational screen of the same target — and require every future MurA docking proposal to state, in its methods, that fosfomycin is covalent and that its docking score is not a binding-affinity benchmark. **If evidence Z appears, reverse this:** if all three candidates lose to fosfomycin in the enzyme assay or fail against C115D, drop this compound set and redirect the same screen to the pyrrolidinedione chemotype, which has already cleared the C115D test — do not proceed to any animal or clinical work on docking scores alone. The Islamic principle is *hifz al-nafs* (preservation of life, a maqsad of the Sharia): where a cheap test can distinguish a usable drug from a useless one for infections that drive a large share of the 1.27 million annual AMR deaths, running the test is the obligation; prescribing on an untested docking score would violate it. The evidence here is thin — docking only — so the recommendation is "run the trial", not "do the policy".
