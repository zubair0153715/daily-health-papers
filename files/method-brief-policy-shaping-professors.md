# Method Brief: Policy-Shaping Professors — the Standard Our Papers Must Chase

**Purpose.** Zubair's daily research papers now cover 5 fields — Health, Brain, Space,
Natural Science, Social Science. This brief sets the bar: the method of the
Harvard / MIT / Oxford (and equivalent) scholars whose papers actually changed
policies, economies and whole fields. Every paper-writing run must read this
first. Every exemplar below is a real scholar with a real, verified paper.

---

## 1. Exemplar scholars — papers that changed the world

### Social science / economics / education

**1. Abhijit Banerjee & Esther Duflo — MIT (J-PAL).**
Landmark: *Poor Economics* (2011, book); the RCT programme behind it; Nobel
Prize in Economics 2019 (with Michael Kremer).
What changed: randomised controlled trials became the default tool of
development economics. J-PAL has run 1,000+ randomised evaluations in 90
countries; its evidence shifted WHO/UN policy toward subsidised preventive
health care, and remedial-tutoring programmes reached 5M+ Indian children.
Lesson: shrink a grand question into one small testable question, randomise,
measure.

**2. Daron Acemoglu, Simon Johnson & James A. Robinson — MIT / U. Chicago.**
Landmark: "The Colonial Origins of Comparative Development: An Empirical
Investigation", *American Economic Review* 91(5), 2001, 1369–1401; *Why
Nations Fail* (2012); Nobel Prize 2024.
What changed: institutions — not geography or culture — became the accepted
explanation of why nations prosper or fail; the settler-mortality
instrumental-variable design rewrote development policy and aid thinking.
Lesson: find a source of variation no one can manipulate (history itself) and
use it to identify a causal effect. Note the honest caveat: Albouy's data
critique was debated openly — landmark papers invite attack and survive it.

**3. Raj Chetty — Harvard (Opportunity Insights).**
Landmark: "The Opportunity Atlas: Mapping the Childhood Roots of Social
Mobility", NBER Working Paper 25147, October 2018 (with Friedman, Hendren,
Jones, Porter).
What changed: built on anonymised records of 20 million Americans, the Atlas
showed a child's neighbourhood causally shapes adult earnings — outcomes vary
sharply between tracts a mile apart. US housing and place-based policy now
targets specific high-opportunity, affordable neighbourhoods instead of
counties. Lesson: publish the DATA as a public tool, not just the finding.

**4. Richard Thaler (U. Chicago) & Cass Sunstein (Harvard Law).**
Landmark: *Nudge: Improving Decisions about Health, Wealth, and Happiness*
(Yale UP, 2008); Thaler, Nobel 2017.
What changed: the UK created the Behavioural Insights Team ("Nudge Unit",
2010); 200+ behavioural-insights teams now exist worldwide; pension
auto-enrolment (opt-out, not opt-in) now covers >90% of eligible UK
private-sector workers. Lesson: a tiny change in choice architecture, tested
by RCT, beats a grand reform. Honest caveat: at-scale nudge effects are far
smaller than journal effects (DellaVigna & Linos, *Econometrica* 2022) —
report the small number, not the exciting one.

**5. James Heckman — U. Chicago.**
Landmark: "Schools, Skills, and Synapses", *Economic Inquiry* 46(3), 2008,
289–324 (the Heckman Curve); Perry Preschool cost–benefit analyses; Nobel 2000.
What changed: 40-year follow-up of a randomised preschool programme showed
6–10% annual return per dollar — better than equities — via non-cognitive
skills, not IQ. Early-childhood investment became a fiscal argument, not a
sentimental one, and reshaped US state pre-K budgets.
Lesson: follow the same people for decades; the mechanism (character skills)
matters more than the headline metric (IQ).

**6. Eric Hanushek — Stanford (Hoover Institution).**
Landmark: "Teacher Characteristics and Gains in Student Achievement" (1971) —
invented teacher value-added measurement; *The Knowledge Capital of Nations*
(MIT Press, 2015, with Woessmann); Yidan Prize 2021.
What changed: teacher quality is now measured by student learning gains
worldwide; school accountability systems, value-added evaluation and the
"skills, not years of schooling, drive growth" finding reshaped education
finance debates in dozens of countries. Lesson: measure the OUTPUT (learning),
not the INPUT (spending).

**7. Dani Rodrik — Harvard (Kennedy School).**
Landmark: *One Economics, Many Recipes* (Princeton UP, 2007); growth
diagnostics with Hausmann & Velasco (2005).
What changed: "growth diagnostics" — find each economy's binding constraint
before prescribing — became standard practice at the World Bank and IMF, and
industrial policy went from heresy to normal policy discussion.
Lesson: no universal recipe; diagnose first, prescribe second.

**8. Angus Deaton — Princeton.**
Landmark: *The Great Escape: Health, Wealth, and the Origins of Inequality*
(2013); Nobel 2015 "for his analysis of consumption, poverty, and welfare".
What changed: his measurement work forced the World Bank to rethink how the
global poverty line and PPPs are built (a 2005 PPP revision moved the 1993
global poor count by ~500 million people); poverty is now argued about with
survey data, not slogans. Lesson: measurement IS the argument — and he is the
RCT movement's sharpest critic, a reminder that no method is above criticism.

### Health

**9. Alex B. Haynes & Atul Gawande — Harvard.**
Landmark: "A Surgical Safety Checklist to Reduce Morbidity and Mortality in a
Global Population", *New England Journal of Medicine* 360, 2009, 491–499.
What changed: in 8 hospitals in 8 countries, a 19-item checklist cut major
complications 11.0% → 7.0% and inpatient deaths 1.5% → 0.8%. WHO adopted it
globally; surgical checklists are now standard practice in operating rooms
worldwide. Honest caveat: Ontario's 101-hospital rollout (Urbach et al., NEJM
2014) found ~no effect — implementation quality, not the paper, decides the
real-world result. Our papers must cite the null replication too.

### Natural science

**10. Stephen Pacala & Robert Socolow — Princeton.**
Landmark: "Stabilization Wedges: Solving the Climate Problem for the Next 50
Years with Current Technologies", *Science* 305(5686), 2004, 968–972.
What changed: the "wedges" framing — seven 1-GtC/yr strategies — became the
standard way governments and the IPCC communicate climate mitigation; it
reframed an impossible problem as a portfolio of deployable pieces.
Honest caveat: 2026 reporting showed BP helped shape the paper — even
landmark work must disclose funding and influence. We disclose everything.

**11. Myles Allen — Oxford (Physics).**
Landmark: "Warming caused by cumulative carbon emissions", *Nature* 458,
2009, 1163–1166.
What changed: proved peak warming depends on CUMULATIVE emissions, not annual
rates — the "trillion-tonne carbon budget". It rewrote climate targets
(carbon budgets in the Paris Agreement era), influenced Shell's $1.35bn CCS
investment decision, and anchors every national net-zero pathway today.
Lesson: one clean quantitative relationship can replace a thousand pages of
targets.

## 2. Their shared method pattern — the checklist every paper run must follow

1. **One falsifiable question.** Not "AI in healthcare" but "does X change Y
   by how much, in whom, versus what?" State the hypothesis AND what result
   would prove it wrong (the falsifier) in the Abstract.
2. **Causal identification, named explicitly.** RCT / natural experiment /
   instrumental variable / difference-in-differences / regression
   discontinuity / re-docking with a validated control (our docking papers).
   If the design cannot support causality, say "association only" in bold.
3. **Mechanism section.** Not just "it works" but WHY — the causal chain,
   with a test for each link (Heckman's non-cognitive skills; Chetty's
   neighbourhood channels; Allen's cumulative-emissions physics).
4. **Effect size + uncertainty, never p-values alone.** Report the number, its
   confidence/credible interval, and the sample or computation behind it
   (e.g. docking ±1–2 kcal/mol noise; RCT effect in percentage points).
5. **Pre-registration or its honest substitute.** State the analysis plan
   BEFORE the results; any post-hoc analysis is labelled exploratory.
6. **Replication & data availability.** Data, code, parameters and negative
   results are published with the paper (Chetty published the Atlas itself).
   A result no one can reproduce is a rumour.
7. **Limitations section that bites.** Name the three biggest threats to the
   conclusion (sample, setting, method) — including null replications that
   exist (Urbach 2014 vs Haynes 2009).
8. **Policy implications section.** Name the decision-maker, the decision,
   and the exact change: "The health ministry should do X instead of Y; if
   evidence Z appears, reverse this." No vague "policymakers should consider".
9. **Comparison against the best alternative.** A new drug, policy or method
   is judged against the strongest incumbent (reference drug re-dock;
   status-quo policy), never against nothing.
10. **One clean figure or number.** Allen's trillion tonnes; Chetty's tract
    map; the checklist's 19 items. If the paper has no single memorable
    number, the argument is not finished.

**What does NOT count:** pure opinion essays; untested frameworks or
"proposed models" with no data (this is exactly why ChemRxiv rejected our
Papers #1 and #3 v1); cherry-picked examples; a literature review dressed as
a discovery; claims without a falsifier; effect sizes without uncertainty.

## 3. Field guides — what "Harvard / MIT / Oxford-level" means per field

### Health (existing)
Bar: NEJM, The Lancet, JAMA, Nature Medicine, BMJ. Means: a result that
would change a guideline or a formulary — benchmarked against the reference
drug on the correct structure, with honest noise bounds. Route: ChemRxiv →
peer review. (Already running: 60+ papers.)

### Brain (existing)
Bar: Nature Neuroscience, Neuron, Brain, Lancet Neurology/Psychiatry. Means:
a mechanistic, falsifiable model of a brain process with a testable
prediction (not a narrative review). Route: medRxiv → peer review.

### Space (existing)
Bar: Nature Astronomy, ApJ, MNRAS, Science. Means: a quantitative prediction
a telescope can kill (e.g. "planet X must show Y in JWST transit, or the
model is wrong"). Route: arXiv → peer review.

### Natural Science (NEW)
Bar: Nature, Science, Nature Climate Change, Nature Energy, PNAS. Means: a
quantitative, physics/chemistry-grounded result with a number a ministry or
an engineer can use (a budget, a rate, a threshold) — Allen's trillion tonnes
is the template. Computation + public data (NASA, NOAA, IEA, open
materials databases) is our route; no lab needed for synthesis papers.
Starter topic seeds (falsifiable, researchable by a Pharm-D student + AI):
1. Pakistan's Indus Basin groundwater: does GRACE satellite data show the
   depletion rate accelerating after 2015, and which districts cross a
   stated threshold first? (Data: NASA GRACE, PCRWR.)
2. Solar + storage vs grid electricity in Larkana: at 2026 panel/battery
   prices, what is the levelised cost crossover, and how many households
   cross it? (Data: NEPRA tariffs, market prices.)
3. Karachi heat: do MODIS land-surface temperatures show the urban heat
   island widening faster than population growth since 2000? (Data: NASA MODIS.)
4. Indus delta mangroves: does Landsat time-series show net loss or net gain
   2000–2025, and does it track freshwater flow at Kotri? (Data: Landsat, WAPDA.)
5. Pakistan's air: does Sentinel-5P NO2 data falsify the claim that Lahore's
   winter smog is mainly transboundary? (Data: ESA Sentinel-5P, EPA Punjab.)

### Social Science (NEW)
Bar: Quarterly Journal of Economics, American Economic Review, Econometrica,
Science, Nature Human Behaviour, Journal of Public Economics. Means: a causal
claim about people or institutions, identified by an RCT, natural experiment
or quasi-experimental design, with an effect size and a named policy lever —
Banerjee/Duflo/Chetty/Heckman are the template. Literature synthesis +
public Pakistani datasets (PSLM, DHS, PMYP disbursement data, SBP, HEC) is
our route; no fieldwork needed for synthesis papers.
Starter topic seeds (falsifiable, researchable by a Pharm-D student + AI):
1. PM Youth Loan (PMYBALS): does district-level disbursement predict
   youth enterprise registrations, or does it track political alignment?
   (Data: PMYP releases, SECP registrations — a falsifiable test.)
2. School stipends: do conditional cash transfers raise girls' secondary
   enrolment in Sindh more than unconditional ones? (Design: difference-in-
   differences on PSLM waves; mechanism: Heckman-style returns.)
3. Health nudges: would default opt-in appointment reminders cut no-shows
   in Pakistani public clinics? (Design: propose an RCT protocol; estimate
   the effect size needed from published no-show baselines — Thaler/Sunstein
   template, no fieldwork.)
4. Science funding: does Pakistan's NRPU/Higher Education funding predict
   publication output, or does output track university size alone?
   (Data: HEC, Scopus — a regression with a falsifiable null.)
5. Teacher value-added in Pakistan: can PSLM + board-exam data support a
   Hanushek-style value-added estimate for districts, and does it beat
   spending-per-pupil as a predictor of exam pass rates?

## 4. Honesty guardrails (non-negotiable)

- Every paper carries: "AI-assisted draft — NOT peer-reviewed, NOT published.
  All claims require independent verification." No exceptions.
- Never claim any professor's endorsement, collaboration or approval. These
  scholars are the STANDARD, not co-authors.
- Never present a draft as equal to their published work. Our papers are
  practice at their method, not their peers.
- Every number traces to a real source (PMID / DOI / dataset) or a real
  computation (docking log, dataset query). No invented citations — the
  ChemRxiv rejections of Papers #1/#3 v1 are the standing warning.
- Negative results and null replications are published, not buried.
- Policy recommendations name the decision-maker and the falsifier; if the
  evidence is thin, the recommendation is "run the trial", not "do the policy".
