## Abstract

**Question.** Most mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI) is scanned and almost nothing is found: only about 16% of head CTs show an intracranial lesion and fewer than 1% of patients need neurosurgery, yet about 82% of emergency patients with mild head injury still receive a CT. We asked whether a blood test measuring two brain proteins — glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and ubiquitin C-terminal hydrolase L1 (UCH-L1) — can safely replace the CT in low-risk patients, and what a Pakistani validation trial would look like. **Claim.** In ALERT-TBI (n = 1,959, 22 centres, *Lancet Neurology* 2018), the combined test detected intracranial injury with 97.6% sensitivity (95% CI 93.1–99.5) and 99.6% negative predictive value (98.7–99.9); only 3 of 1,959 patients were CT-positive despite a negative test, and none needed neurosurgery. A 2025 meta-analysis of 16 studies (*Annals of Emergency Medicine* 2026) pooled sensitivity at 100% (99–100) with specificity 31% (26–36), implying about 31% of CTs could be avoided. The US FDA has cleared four platforms (Banyan 2018; Abbott i-STAT plasma 2021; Alinity i 2023; i-STAT whole blood 2024, 24-hour window). In Pakistan, where WHO estimates about 28,000 road deaths a year and one Karachi hospital alone sees about 56.5 head-injury patients a day, a biomarker-first pathway could avoid roughly 310 CTs per 1,000 mild-TBI patients and save PKR 310,000–3.4 million at current CT prices — but no Pakistani patient has ever been tested. We pre-specify a 1,200-patient, six-centre validation trial. **Falsifier.** Sensitivity below 95%, CT reduction below 10%, or any missed neurosurgical lesion in a biomarker-negative patient refutes the triage claim. **Identification.** Diagnostic-accuracy evidence from prospective cohorts; **association only** for the claim that fewer CTs improve outcomes — no trial has yet shown it. **One clean number: 99.6%** — the negative predictive value.

**Keywords:** traumatic brain injury; GFAP; UCH-L1; biomarkers; CT triage; emergency medicine; Pakistan; road traffic injury

## 1. Introduction

About 69 million people sustain a traumatic brain injury (TBI) every year worldwide, and about 81% of them are mild — a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 13–15 (Dewan et al., *Journal of Neurosurgery*, 2019). The Global Burden of Disease study, counting only cases that reach hospitals and registries, puts the number lower, at 27.16 million new cases in 2019 — the gap between the two estimates is itself a measure of how much mild injury never reaches care. Mild TBI is therefore the commonest brain injury and the most commonly mishandled one, because the clinical problem is not the injury itself but the decision that follows it: who needs a head CT, and who can safely go home?

The CT is the strongest incumbent in emergency medicine, and it is a blunt one. Only about 16% of patients with mild TBI show an intracranial lesion on CT, and fewer than 1% require neurosurgical intervention — yet in comparable US emergency departments about 82% of these patients receive a brain CT, and roughly 91% of those scans are negative. Every negative scan costs money, occupies a scanner, keeps a patient in the department, and delivers about 2 mSv of ionising radiation. Clinical decision rules were built to trim this waste: the Canadian CT Head Rule, derived in 3,121 patients across ten Canadian emergency departments (Stiell et al., *The Lancet*, 2001), is 100% sensitive for injuries needing neurosurgery and 98.4% sensitive for clinically important brain injury, and applying it would send about 54% of eligible patients to CT instead of 82%. But even the best rule is a checklist of symptoms and signs — it cannot see inside the skull, and in practice 10–35% of CTs obtained for mild TBI are not recommended by any guideline. The rule's specificity is only 28–65%: it still sends many uninjured patients to the scanner, because a rule for brain bleeding must err on the side of catching everyone.

A blood test offers a different kind of evidence: proteins that leak from damaged brain cells into the bloodstream. GFAP is a structural protein of astrocytes, the support cells that wrap the brain's blood vessels; UCH-L1 is an enzyme found in neurons. When the brain is injured, both appear in blood within an hour — GFAP rising and falling over about a day, UCH-L1 earlier and more briefly. The idea is the troponin analogy: just as troponin in blood proves heart-muscle injury, GFAP and UCH-L1 in blood prove brain injury, and their absence — within the right time window — proves there is nothing for a CT to find.

The classical physicians already practised this decision, with the tools they had. Ibn Sina's *Canon of Medicine* (c. 1025) devotes a detailed chapter, "Fracture of the Skull", to head injuries, systematising the signs that distinguish a fracture that must be opened from one that must be left alone (Aciduman et al., *Neurosurgical Review*, 2009). Al-Zahrawi, in the surgical volume of his *Kitab al-Tasrif* (c. 1000), wrote the triage rule in plain words: if the patient shows serious signs — high fever, repeated vomiting, protruding eyes, convulsions, coma — "do not touch him because he is probably going to die"; otherwise, treat him by the steps he then describes, with instruments designed not to penetrate beyond the skull. The principle is identical to the modern one: read the injury from observable signals, operate only when the signals demand it, and do no harm by intervening where there is nothing to fix. A blood test is the same triage, read from molecules instead of symptoms. The canon is a hypothesis source for this paper, not evidence — it knew nothing of GFAP — but the continuity of the clinical question across a thousand years is worth stating.

The question matters most where the CT is scarcest. Pakistan loses about 28,000 people a year to road crashes (WHO estimate for 2021, 11.9 per 100,000 population), with the Global Burden of Disease study estimating higher, about 38,000, and national statistics recording only about 5,000 — an undercount that itself shows the surveillance gap. Road crashes cost Pakistan about USD 12 billion in 2021, roughly 3% of GDP, more than the country's entire health expenditure that year. At Jinnah Postgraduate Medical Centre (JPMC) in Karachi, 5,546 patients with TBI were seen in the neurosurgical emergency department in about four months (September–December 2019) — about 56.5 patients a day, 73.1% male, 26% under the age of ten, road traffic accidents the commonest cause (39%), and 72.8% of injuries mild. In a multicentre surveillance study across seven Pakistani teaching hospitals (Pak-NEDS), a CT was performed in only 40% of TBI patients — not because the rest were low-risk, but because scanners, staff and money run out. A test that safely says "no scan needed" is, in that setting, not a convenience but access.

This paper's contribution is threefold: (i) a quantitative synthesis of the published diagnostic-accuracy evidence for GFAP + UCH-L1, with every figure traced to its source, including a correction of a common misattribution; (ii) a computed illustration of what the test would be worth in a Pakistani emergency department, with every assumption stated; and (iii) a fully pre-specified validation trial design for Pakistan, including a sample size derived from the published numbers, that would decide whether the test works in Pakistani patients — because no Pakistani patient has ever been tested, and an imported American result is not a Pakistani result.

**Falsifiable question.** In Pakistani emergency departments, does a GFAP + UCH-L1 blood test drawn within 12 hours of mild TBI safely reduce head CT use by at least 20% without missing a neurosurgically significant injury? **Falsifier:** a prospective Pakistani validation study showing sensitivity below 95% for intracranial injury, or CT reduction below 10%, or any missed neurosurgical lesion in a biomarker-negative patient, refutes the triage claim. **Causal identification:** diagnostic-accuracy evidence from prospective cohorts for the test's performance; **association only** for the claim that fewer CTs improve patient outcomes — no randomised trial has yet shown that a biomarker-first pathway changes outcomes, and this paper does not pretend otherwise.

### 2.2 Synthesis methods

No new patient data were collected and no trial was run. Every number in §3 is either quoted from a cited publication or computed from quoted numbers by arithmetic shown in the text. Pooled figures are the publications' own pooled analyses, not a new meta-analysis by this author — this paper did not re-pool raw data it does not hold. Where a comparison crosses studies (the Pakistan illustration in §3.5), it is labelled an illustration with stated assumptions, not a trial result, and its direction of bias is stated.

### 2.3 Computations performed in this paper

Four computations were performed from published numbers. (a) JPMC annualised volume: 56.5 TBI patients per day × 365 ≈ 20,600 TBI patients per year at one hospital; mild 72.8% → ≈ 15,000 mild-TBI patients per year. (b) CT-avoidance illustration: per 1,000 mild-TBI patients who would all be scanned, the biomarker-negative share is 31% (Puravet pooled specificity) to 34% (ALERT-TBI negative share, 671/1,959) → 310–340 CTs avoided; at PKR 1,000 (JPMC public brain CT) to PKR 10,000 (private Lahore CT brain) → PKR 310,000–3,400,000 gross saving per 1,000 patients, before the cost of the test — and no Pakistani price for the test exists, so the net saving cannot be computed and is not claimed. (c) Radiation avoided: ≈ 310 avoided CTs × ≈ 2 mSv per head CT ≈ 620 mSv collective dose avoided per 1,000 patients. (d) Sample size for the proposed trial (§3.6): assuming a CT-positive prevalence of 10% among mild-TBI patients presenting to Pakistani emergency departments (a conservative assumption — the published lesion rate is about 16%, but Pakistani emergency cohorts include many lower-risk walk-in patients), n = 1,200 evaluable patients yields about 120 CT-positive cases; an observed sensitivity of 97.5% in 120 cases carries a 95% Wilson lower bound of about 93%. For the CT-reduction endpoint, an expected avoidance of 31% in 1,200 patients carries a 95% confidence interval of 28.4–33.6% — comfortably above the 20% decision threshold. Enrolment is inflated to 1,320 for 10% invalid samples or loss to follow-up.

### 2.4 Analysis plan (stated before results)

The plan was fixed before any number was written down: report diagnostic accuracy from the prospective trials and meta-analyses only; report the regulatory timeline as fact, not as evidence of clinical value; choose head CT — not "no test" and not the Canadian CT Head Rule alone — as the comparator, because CT is the incumbent that the blood test must displace; compute the Pakistan illustration only as an illustration with its assumptions stated; and state the falsifier before the conclusion.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 ALERT-TBI: the trial that proved a negative result can be trusted

ALERT-TBI (A Prospective Clinical Evaluation of Biomarkers of Traumatic Brain Injury) enrolled 1,959 adults with suspected non-penetrating TBI (GCS 9–15) at 22 emergency departments in the USA and Europe between December 2012 and March 2014, drew blood within 12 hours of injury, and compared a combined GFAP + UCH-L1 test — positive if either protein was at or above its pre-set cutoff (GFAP 22 pg/mL, UCH-L1 327 pg/mL) — against head CT (Bazarian et al., *Lancet Neurology*, 2018). The result: sensitivity 97.6% (95% CI 93.1–99.5) and negative predictive value 99.6% (95% CI 98.7–99.9) for intracranial injury on CT, with specificity 36.4%. Of the 1,959 patients, 671 (34%) tested negative — and in only three of the whole cohort was the CT positive when the test was negative (3/1,959 = 0.15%). The test was 100% sensitive, with 100% negative predictive value, for neurosurgically manageable lesions — the injuries that actually kill. Median GFAP was 135.0 pg/mL in CT-positive patients versus 22.2 pg/mL in CT-negative patients; median UCH-L1 was 604.8 versus 261.0 pg/mL (both p < 0.0001). The trial was funded by Banyan Biomarkers, maker of the first authorised assay, and the US Army — a funding fact the reader should know, and one the trial's prospective, pre-registered-cutoff design partly answers. The honest summary: a negative test within 12 hours means a CT will almost certainly be normal, and the residual risk — 3 missed lesions in 1,959 patients, none needing surgery — is the number any adoption decision must weigh against the harm of scanning everyone.

### 3.2 The 2025 meta-analysis: the pooled number is stronger, and the weakness is clearer

Puravet et al. (*Annals of Emergency Medicine*, 2026;87:167–180; PROSPERO CRD42024562587) screened 379 articles and pooled 16 studies of combined GFAP + UCH-L1 after mild TBI in adults. The pooled sensitivity was 100% (95% CI 99–100) and pooled specificity 31% (95% CI 26–36), with a pooled negative predictive value of 100% and an area under the curve of 97%. GFAP alone pooled at 94% sensitivity (91–97) and 40% specificity (34–46); UCH-L1 alone at 83% (69–94) and 51% (40–63). The authors' conclusion, quoted in substance: routine use of the combined assay could theoretically reduce cranial CT scans by 31%. A second 2025 meta-analysis (14 studies) found similar performance at optimised cutoffs, and a 2025 umbrella meta-analysis of all mTBI biomarkers put S100B — the older European rule-out marker, already in the Scandinavian guideline — at 91.6% sensitivity and 42.4% specificity, GFAP at 84.5% and 61.0%, and UCH-L1 at 86.7% and 37.3%. Two things follow. First, the combined test is a rule-out test, not a rule-in test: its value is the negative result, and its specificity of about one in three is the price of that safety — roughly two of every three patients who test positive will still have a normal CT, and they must still be scanned. Second, GFAP alone performs nearly as well as the pair, and the incremental value of UCH-L1 beyond GFAP is small in the 2025 narrative review's reading; every cleared platform nevertheless measures both, because that is what the trials validated. A test with 100% pooled sensitivity and 31% specificity is not a perfect test. It is a test that trades two-thirds of its positive results for a negative result you can trust — and for a triage question, that is the right trade.

### 3.3 Regulation: four clearances in six years, and what each one actually covers

The regulatory timeline is fast by medical-device standards, and each step narrows the gap between the laboratory and the bedside. The Banyan Brain Trauma Indicator was authorised through the FDA's De Novo pathway on 14 February 2018 (DEN170045; 21 CFR 866.5830) — the first blood test for evaluating suspected mild TBI — but it was never commercialised: its chemiluminescent ELISA took 3–4 hours, too slow for an emergency department, and it survives as the regulatory predicate for everything after it. The Abbott i-STAT TBI plasma test was cleared in January 2021 (510(k) K201778): a cartridge for the handheld i-STAT analyser, results in about 15 minutes, but requiring centrifuged plasma in a clinical laboratory — portable in form, not in practice. The Abbott Alinity i laboratory test followed on 7 March 2023, the first commercially available lab-based assay. The Abbott i-STAT TBI whole-blood test was cleared in April 2024: unprocessed whole blood, at the bedside, in about 15 minutes, and — the change that matters most for Pakistan — the cleared window extended from 12 to 24 hours after injury. bioMérieux's VIDAS TBI assay received CE marking in 2023 and FDA 510(k) clearance in 2024. In the FDA validation cohorts, the i-STAT plasma test showed 95.8% sensitivity and 40.4% specificity (n = 1,901), and the whole-blood test 96.5% and 40.3% (n = 420) — slightly below the ALERT-TBI figures, as validation cohorts usually are. Every clearance carries the same limit, and it must be stated in full: these tests are cleared only as an aid, used alongside other clinical information, in adults with suspected mild TBI (GCS 13–15) within 12 hours of injury (24 hours for the whole-blood cartridge). None is cleared to diagnose a concussion. None is cleared for children. None is registered with Pakistan's Drug Regulatory Authority, as far as this search could establish — and no Pakistani price exists.

### 3.4 Comparison against the strongest incumbent: the CT, and the rule that already rations it

The honest comparator is the head CT itself, and the honest second comparator is the Canadian CT Head Rule, which already rations CTs without any blood test. Against the CT, the biomarker's case is arithmetic: the CT finds injury in about 16% of mild-TBI patients and misses nothing it is asked to find, but it costs PKR 1,000–10,000 in Pakistan, delivers about 2 mSv, and — in most Pakistani hospitals — is not available at all at 2 a.m. Against the Canadian CT Head Rule, the biomarker's case is narrower and must be stated as such: the rule is already 100% sensitive for neurosurgical injury and sends about 54% of patients to CT instead of 82%, with no blood test, no cartridge, and no supply chain. The biomarker's advantage over the rule is that it is a measurement, not a checklist — it does not depend on whether the patient remembers the accident, whether the clinician asks about vomiting, or whether the patient's GCS was 15 at two hours. The 2025 narrative review's proposal is the right shape: a sequential algorithm — the Canadian CT Head Rule first, biomarker testing in rule-positive cases, CT reserved for biomarker-positive patients — which could cut scanning further than either tool alone, though integrated approaches "remain poorly characterized in the literature" and no trial has yet tested the sequence. Against "scan everyone", the biomarker wins easily and is therefore the wrong comparison to headline — a win against the worst incumbent is not evidence. This paper's trial design (§3.6) is built around the hard comparison: biomarker-first versus current practice, with CT reduction and missed injuries both measured, not assumed.

### 3.5 Pakistan: the arithmetic of one hospital, and what it cannot prove

At JPMC, the arithmetic is stark. About 56.5 TBI patients a day is about 20,600 a year at a single hospital, of whom about 15,000 are mild (§2.3a). In the Pak-NEDS multicentre study, a CT was performed in only 40% of TBI patients; the other 60% were managed without imaging — some because they were low-risk, many because the scanner was busy, broken, or unaffordable. The cost of a CT brain is PKR 1,000 at JPMC itself, Rs 2,500 for a plain CT at the Rawalpindi allied hospitals' outpatient departments, and PKR 10,000 at a private Lahore laboratory (2026 prices; contrast adds PKR 5,000–8,000). Apply the biomarker's numbers as an illustration (§2.3b): per 1,000 mild-TBI patients who would all be scanned, 310–340 CTs avoided, a gross saving of PKR 310,000–3,400,000, and about 620 mSv of collective radiation dose avoided (§2.3c). At JPMC's own volume — 15,000 mild-TBI patients a year — that illustration scales to roughly 4,650–5,100 CTs avoided a year at one hospital. Three caveats, stated plainly. First, the saving is gross, not net: the test's price in Pakistan does not exist, and if a cartridge costs more than the CT it replaces, the arithmetic reverses — the paper does not claim a saving it cannot price. Second, the 31% figure is a theoretical reduction from pooled specificity, not a measured reduction in any hospital; real-world adherence, invalid samples, and patients arriving after the 12-hour window (24 hours for whole blood) will all shrink it. Third, the largest Pakistani benefit is not in Karachi at all: it is in the district hospital with no CT, where a negative blood test means a patient can safely stay, and a positive one means a transfer that is genuinely needed — and that benefit has never been measured anywhere, because no trial has run in a hospital without a scanner.

### 3.6 The trial that would settle it

We pre-specify a prospective, multicentre diagnostic-accuracy and implementation trial in Pakistan — **design:** 1,200 evaluable mild-TBI patients (GCS 13–15, blood drawn within 12 hours, whole-blood cartridge where available) across six tertiary emergency departments (JPMC Karachi, Aga Khan University Hospital Karachi, Lady Reading Hospital Peshawar, Benazir Bhutto Hospital Rawalpindi, Services Hospital Lahore, and one district-level hospital without 24-hour CT), with head CT as the reference standard in every patient for the accuracy phase, then a stepped-wedge implementation phase in which the biomarker-first pathway replaces CT-first practice centre by centre. **Co-primary endpoints:** (i) sensitivity for intracranial injury on CT, with a pre-specified success threshold of a 95% lower confidence bound above 90%; (ii) proportion of CTs avoided, with a success threshold of 20%. **Sample size:** 1,200 evaluable patients (200 per centre), inflated to 1,320 enrolled for 10% invalid samples or loss to follow-up — sized by the sensitivity endpoint (§2.3d), with the CT-reduction endpoint's confidence interval (28.4–33.6%) comfortably above its threshold. **Secondary endpoints:** missed neurosurgical lesions (a safety endpoint with a stopping rule — a single missed neurosurgical lesion in a biomarker-negative patient halts the implementation phase), time from arrival to disposition, transfer rates from the district site, out-of-pocket cost per patient, and 30-day functional outcome. **Falsifier:** sensitivity below 95%, CT reduction below 10%, or any missed neurosurgical lesion in a biomarker-negative patient refutes the triage claim. **Cost note:** no trial cost is stated, because the per-test price in Pakistan does not exist — the funder must price the cartridge first, and that price is itself a finding the trial should report.

## 4. Discussion

**Mechanism.** Why do two proteins in blood tell you what a CT would show? GFAP is released when astrocytes are damaged — the support cells that wrap the brain's blood vessels, so their injury marks vascular and parenchymal damage together; UCH-L1 is released when neurons themselves are damaged or die, and it appears slightly earlier but fades within about eight hours. Running them together covers two kinds of damage with two different time courses, which is why the pair outperforms either alone in the meta-analysis (pooled sensitivity 100% versus 94% for GFAP alone and 83% for UCH-L1 alone). The mechanism predicts exactly the profile observed: a test that is exquisitely sensitive in the first hours, loses its negative predictive value as the proteins clear, and cannot distinguish a small contusion from a large one — it answers one question, "is there anything for a CT to find?", and no other. The prediction held; the mechanism is not merely a story. But the mechanism also predicts the test's limits: a patient who arrives after the window, a patient with an extracranial injury that raises GFAP, a child (in whom the test is not cleared), and a patient whose injury is functional rather than structural — none of these is answered by the blood test, and the paper does not pretend otherwise.

**Comparison against the strongest incumbent.** The CT is the incumbent, and it has one property no blood test will ever have: it shows the injury. The biomarker's claim is narrower — it shows the absence of injury, within a window, with a stated error rate of about 3 missed lesions per 2,000 patients, none of them surgical. Against the Canadian CT Head Rule, the biomarker's advantage is that it is a measurement rather than a checklist, and its disadvantage is that it needs a cartridge, a cold chain, and a price. The right policy is therefore not "replace the CT" but "sequence the tools": rule first, blood second, CT last — and measure whether the sequence holds in Pakistani hands before any guideline changes.

**What the evidence cannot yet say.** The outcome claim is **association only**. The chain has three links — the test accurately rules out injury; ruling out injury avoids CTs; avoiding CTs improves outcomes (less radiation, shorter stays, lower cost, fewer transfers) — and only the first link is supported by prospective data. The second is a theoretical reduction from specificity, not a measured one. The third has never been tested: no randomised trial has shown that a biomarker-first pathway changes what happens to patients, and the one outcome that matters most — a missed injury that bleeds later — is rare enough that only a large implementation trial with a stopping rule can measure it. A 99.6% negative predictive value is a diagnostic fact, not a health outcome, and this paper's claim is deliberately narrow: the test deserves a Pakistani validation trial, not a Pakistani guideline change today.

**The Islamic reading.** The canon supplies the frame, not the data. Ibn Sina's chapter on skull fracture and Al-Zahrawi's triage rule show that the classical physicians faced the same decision — whom to open, whom to leave — and answered it by reading observable signals with discipline. Al-Razi's insistence on observation over authority is the argument for running the trial in Pakistan rather than importing the American result on faith: a claim must be tested where it will be used. The principle behind acting on the evidence is *hifz al-nafs* — preservation of life, one of the five objectives of the law (*maqasid al-shariah*) as defined by Al-Ghazali in *Al-Mustasfa* and systematised by Al-Shatibi in *Al-Muwafaqat* (d. 1388) — and the Qur'anic injunction that saving one life is as saving all mankind (Qur'an 5:32). Where the canon and the modern evidence point the same way, this paper says so: both demand that the injured brain be protected without wasting the resources that protect the next patient. Where they differ, honesty requires saying so too: the canon offers no guidance on a cartridge price, and the price question is answered by *'adl* (justice, Qur'an 4:135; 16:90) and *maslaha* (public interest) as principles — a test that only the private Karachi laboratory can afford fails both, and the paper's policy section is built around that test.

## 5. Limitations

1. **No Pakistani data exist.** Every accuracy number in this paper comes from US and European patients. Pakistani patients differ — in injury mechanism (more road traffic, more falls from height, more paediatric injury), in time to hospital, and in the prevalence of CT-positive injury — and any of those differences could move sensitivity. The trial in §3.6 is the answer, not an afterthought.
2. **The pooled 100% sensitivity is a meta-analytic artefact of small studies.** Sixteen studies pooled to 100% (95% CI 99–100) — but a pooled estimate of 100% means no false negatives were observed in the pooled sample, not that false negatives do not exist. ALERT-TBI, the largest single study, found three. The honest number to quote is 97.6%, not 100%.
3. **The 31% CT reduction is theoretical.** It is computed from pooled specificity, not measured in any hospital. Real-world adherence, invalid samples, late arrivals and clinician distrust will all shrink it, and the paper's 20% trial threshold is chosen to allow for exactly that shrinkage.
4. **The test is an aid, not a diagnosis.** It is cleared only for adults with suspected mild TBI within 12 hours (24 hours for whole blood), only alongside other clinical information, and not for children — who are 26% of JPMC's TBI patients. A policy built on this test leaves a quarter of the patients out.
5. **No cost-effectiveness analysis is possible yet.** The test has no Pakistani price, no DRAP registration, and no local distributor as far as this search could establish. The PKR illustration in §3.5 is gross, not net, and a cartridge dearer than a CT would reverse it.
6. **The outcome claim is association only.** No trial links biomarker-first triage to better patient outcomes. The chain from fewer CTs to better health is plausible and unproven.
7. **The CT-availability data for Pakistan are old.** The only national scanner count found is from 2003 (80 scanners, 26 in the public sector) — more than two decades old. No current national count of CT scanners, or of hospitals without one, could be verified; the paper states the gap rather than filling it with a guess.
8. **Islamic-canon limits.** The Canon's head-injury chapter and Al-Zahrawi's surgical volume were consulted through the modern historical reviews of Aciduman et al. (2009) and Ghannaee Arani et al. (2012), not read in full Arabic text; Al-Razi's *Kitab al-Hawi* exists in English only in selections, the full 23 volumes being Arabic; many classical Arabic manuscripts on medicine remain undigitised in libraries such as the Süleymaniye in Istanbul and Dar al-Kutub in Cairo, with no online copy. A paper that read the relevant classical works through their modern reviews and names the ones it could not access is honest; a claim to have read everything would not be.
9. **Radiation and price figures are current snapshots.** The PKR 1,000–10,000 CT range is from 2025–2026 Pakistani sources and will move with inflation; the ~2 mSv head-CT dose is a standard catalogue value, not a measurement in any Pakistani scanner.

## 6. Policy implications

**Decision-maker 1: the Drug Regulatory Authority of Pakistan (DRAP) and the Ministry of National Health Services, Regulations & Coordination.** Do not register any GFAP/UCH-L1 test for routine use on US data alone. Instead, grant a conditional registration for the whole-blood platform — the only one that works at the bedside without a centrifuge and within a 24-hour window — tied to the six-centre validation trial specified in §3.6, with a Pakistani site and Pakistani patients as a condition of full registration; and require the manufacturer to publish a Pakistani price before the trial begins, because a price the public system cannot pay is a finding, not a detail. **Falsifier for this recommendation:** if the trial shows sensitivity below 95% or a missed neurosurgical lesion, the conditional registration is withdrawn and CT-first practice stands.

**Decision-maker 2: provincial health departments (Sindh, Punjab, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Balochistan) and the medical superintendents of tertiary hospitals.** Fund the validation trial, and — in parallel, without waiting for it — adopt the Canadian CT Head Rule as a mandatory checklist item in every emergency department that sees head injury, on the Haynes–Gawande checklist model: it costs nothing, it is already 100% sensitive for neurosurgical injury, and it does not depend on any new device. Where a hospital has no CT, the rule plus a biomarker (once validated) is the triage pathway; where a hospital has a CT, the rule plus a biomarker is the rationing pathway. The Islamic principle is *'adl* (justice, Qur'an 4:135; 16:90) and *maslaha* (public interest): the test must reach the district hospital, not only the private laboratory, or it fails both tests — and the district site in the trial exists precisely to prove the test works where the scanner does not.

**Decision-maker 3: funders of health research (Higher Education Commission, Pakistan Science Foundation, WHO country office).** Fund the validation trial before any guideline changes. The trial needs 1,200 patients across six centres — a small study against the scale of the problem, about 28,000 road deaths a year and about 15,000 mild-TBI patients a year at a single Karachi hospital. If the evidence is thin, the recommendation is "run the trial", not "do the policy".

## 7. Conclusion

Mild traumatic brain injury is the commonest brain injury, and its management is mostly the management of a decision: who needs a CT, and who can safely go home. The evidence supports a precise, limited claim: a blood test measuring GFAP and UCH-L1, drawn within 12 hours of injury, detects intracranial injury with 97.6% sensitivity and a 99.6% negative predictive value (ALERT-TBI, n = 1,959), and a 2025 meta-analysis of 16 studies pools sensitivity at 100% with specificity 31% — implying about 31% of head CTs could be avoided, at the price of scanning two of every three patients who test positive. Four platforms are FDA-cleared, the newest running on whole blood at the bedside in about 15 minutes, up to 24 hours after injury. In Pakistan, where one Karachi hospital alone sees about 56.5 head-injury patients a day and a CT costs PKR 1,000–10,000 — where a CT exists at all — the illustration is worth about 310 avoided scans per 1,000 mild-TBI patients. What the evidence does not yet support is use in Pakistan: no Pakistani patient has ever been tested, no price exists, and no outcome trial has shown that fewer CTs make patients better. The next step is a single, pre-specified, 1,200-patient, six-centre validation trial. If it shows sensitivity above 95% and CT reduction above 20% with no missed surgical lesion, the blood test belongs in the Pakistani emergency department — and the thousand-year-old question Ibn Sina and Al-Zahrawi asked, whom to open and whom to leave, gets a new answer, read from a drop of blood. If it does not, the claim fails cleanly, and that failure is itself the result.
