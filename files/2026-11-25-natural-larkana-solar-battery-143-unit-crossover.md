## Abstract

On 9 February 2026, NEPRA replaced Pakistan's net-metering regime with net billing: a household that exports solar electricity is now credited about Rs 8.13 per unit, while the same unit bought back from the grid costs Rs 28–48 depending on the consumption slab. That 4-to-6-fold spread turned the home battery from a luxury into an arbitrage machine — but only for some households. We model a 5 kW rooftop system with a 5 kWh LiFePO₄ battery in Larkana, Sindh (5.0 kWh/kWp/day), using the notified 2026 tariff schedule (S.R.O. 279(I)/2026), the September 2026 fuel charge, and November 2026 market prices (panels Rs 37–47/W; 5 kW on-grid turn-key Rs 700,000–800,000; hybrid premium +Rs 275,000). **Falsifiable claim:** for an unprotected household, the battery add-on pays for itself within its 10-year warranted life if and only if monthly consumption is at least ~143 units; protected households never cross, at any consumption up to 700 units/month. **Falsifier:** a metered field trial of 30 Larkana hybrid households showing median marginal battery payback above 10 years at 300–400 units/month, or a NEPRA export rate above ~Rs 25/unit, kills the claim. **Causal identification: natural experiment + deterministic cost model** — the 9 February 2026 regulatory switch is the treatment; household-level realised paybacks remain to be measured, so no causal effect on adoption is claimed. **The memorable number: 143 units/month** — the consumption line below which a battery destroys value and above which it creates it (range 117–169 across the observed battery-price range). A 400-unit household earns Rs 35.46 on every unit it stores instead of exporting, pays the battery back in 5.25 years, and gains Rs 401,506 in 25-year net present value over on-grid solar alone.

**Keywords:** rooftop solar; net billing; battery storage; LiFePO₄; NEPRA; Larkana; levelised cost; energy policy; Pakistan

## 1. Introduction

Pakistan is one of the sunniest countries on earth — 300+ sunny days a year, average daily irradiance 4.5–7 kWh/m², up to 6.5 kWh/m²/day in Sindh and Balochistan — and one of the most expensive places to buy grid electricity. The two facts collided in February 2026. For a decade, net metering let a rooftop-solar household bank daytime surplus with the grid at parity: one unit out, one unit credited back. On 9 February 2026 the National Electric Power Regulatory Authority (NEPRA) notified the NEPRA (Prosumer) Regulations 2026, S.R.O. 251(I)/2026, ending that regime. Surplus is now bought at the national average energy purchase price — Rs 8.13 per unit for April–June 2026 (S.R.O. 1501(I)/2026) — while imports are billed at the full slab tariff, Rs 22.44–47.20 per unit before adjustments. The Pakistan Institute of Development Economics (PIDE) estimates the switch cut distribution companies' revenue exposure from rooftop exports from Rs 52.93 billion to Rs 21.97 billion, and stretched the payback of a typical import-reliant 10 kW residential system from 4–5 years to 7–9 years at 60% self-consumption.

That is the problem this paper solves. The PIDE brief and the existing techno-economic literature treat the battery as a footnote. A July 2026 peer-reviewed study of four residential configurations in Karachi found the full-battery-backup case had the highest net present value (USD 25,104, ~4-year payback) while the net-metered case had the lowest levelised cost (USD 0.0307/kWh) — but it did not ask *for whom* the battery pays, or where the threshold lies. The threshold matters because Pakistan's tariff is not one price but a ladder: a protected household using 150 units pays Rs 13.01 per unit; its unprotected neighbour using the same 150 units pays Rs 28.91. The value of a stored unit — import tariff minus export rate — therefore differs by a factor of three between two identical houses on the same street. A policy that tells every household to "add a battery" would push the poorest households into a purchase that never pays back.

Larkana is the right place to settle the question. It sits in upper Sindh, where solar output runs 5.0–5.5 kWh/kWp/day; its distribution company, SEPCO, serves a rice belt whose mills run 18–22 hours a day in peak season; and its households face the full national tariff ladder. This paper computes, from regulated prices and observed market prices alone, the exact consumption threshold at which a battery stops being a cost and starts being an asset — and the policy that follows from it.

The method ancestor is older than the grid. Ibn al-Haytham's *Kitab al-Manazir* (c. 1011–1021) established that claims about nature are settled by controlled observation, not authority. Al-Biruni measured the Earth's circumference to within ~1% by combining geometry with one careful observation, and recorded the sun's behaviour over Sindh itself in *Kitab al-Hind* (c. 1030). The stewardship frame comes from the same tradition: the Qur'an names the human being *khalifah* — trustee — on the earth (2:30), and forbids wasteful excess, *israf* (7:31). Seyyed Hossein Nasr's *Man and Nature* (1968) argues the ecological crisis is at root a spiritual one: nature treated as a resource to exhaust rather than a trust to keep. A battery that lets a household use its own sunlight instead of selling it for Rs 8 and buying it back for Rs 44 is, in that frame, the opposite of *israf* — but only if the household can afford the trust in the first place.

## 2. Methods

**Analysis plan (stated before results).** Five household archetypes, defined by monthly consumption and tariff category, were fixed before any calculation: 100 units unprotected, 150 units unprotected, 200 units protected, 400 units unprotected, 700 units unprotected. For each, we compute annual bill savings, simple payback, and the *marginal* battery payback (hybrid premium divided by the extra annual saving the battery creates) for a 5 kW on-grid system versus the same system plus a 5 kWh battery. The crossover is the smallest monthly consumption at which marginal battery payback falls within the battery's 10-year warranted life, found by scanning 50–700 units/month. A 25-year net present value (NPV) comparison and three sensitivity analyses (export rate, battery premium, battery life) follow. All computation is in `hidden_files/larkana_solar_crossover.py`; every input below is traceable to a cited source.

**Solar resource.** Larkana yield is taken as 5.0 kWh/kWp/day — the conservative end of the 5.0–5.5 kWh/kWp/day range reported for upper Sindh, and consistent with the Sindh Solar Energy Project's Global Solar Atlas figures for the province (global tilted irradiance 5.7–6.4 kWh/m²/day; PV output 1,400–2,000 kWh/kWp/year). A 5 kW system therefore generates 25.0 kWh/day, 9,125 kWh/year. Sensitivity: 4.5–5.5 kWh/kWp/day.

**Load model.** Daily demand is monthly consumption ÷ 30. Of that demand, 40% falls in solar hours (direct self-consumption) and 60% at night (the battery's addressable load). This 40/60 split is a stated assumption, not a measurement; it is the standard daytime/night-time split for a Pakistani home without daytime air-conditioning, and §3 shows it is the largest single uncertainty in the threshold: the crossover moves from 123 to 247 units/month as the night share moves from 70% to 30%.

**Battery model.** 5 kWh LiFePO₄, 90% depth of discharge, 90% round-trip efficiency → at most 4.05 kWh/day delivered. One full cycle per day. Warranted life 10 years (manufacturer cycle rating 6,000 cycles implies ~16 years at one cycle per day; the 10-year warranty is the conservative decision horizon).

**Prices (all November 2026 unless stated).** Grid import: base tariff from S.R.O. 279(I)/2026 — unprotected Rs 22.44 (1–100 units), 28.91 (101–200), 33.10 (201–300), 37.99 (301–400), 40.22 (401–500), 41.62 (501–600), 42.76 (601–700), 47.20 (above 700); protected Rs 10.54 (1–100), 13.01 (101–200) — plus the September 2026 fuel charge adjustment of Rs 2.3673/unit (S.R.O. 2094(I)/2026) and the debt-servicing surcharge (Rs 3.23/unit unprotected, Rs 0.43 protected). The "all-in" marginal unit value is base + fuel charge + surcharge; 18% GST is excluded throughout, which understates savings and is therefore conservative. Export: Rs 8.13/unit (S.R.O. 1501(I)/2026), with sensitivity at Rs 11 (press reports) and Rs 25.32 (the old net-metering rate). System costs: 5 kW on-grid turn-key Rs 750,000 (observed range Rs 700,000–800,000); hybrid premium (5 kWh bank + hybrid inverter upgrade) +Rs 275,000, giving a hybrid total of Rs 1,025,000. Fixed monthly charges (Rs 200–675/kW of sanctioned load) are excluded because they are unchanged by solar adoption and cancel out of the savings comparison.

**NPV assumptions.** 25-year horizon; discount rate 10%; grid tariff escalation 5%/year; export-rate escalation 3%/year; panel degradation 0.5%/year; operation and maintenance 1% of capital cost per year; battery bank replaced in year 11 at Rs 131,000 (50% of today's bank cost). These are stated assumptions, varied in §3.

**Literature sweep.** The latest daily research digest (2026-11-14) contained no solar or net-billing entries (it was bio-focused), so the literature leg rests on targeted searches of 25 November 2026: the NEPRA regulations and tariff notifications, the PIDE Knowledge Brief, the July 2026 Karachi techno-economic study, and the November 2026 market price trackers. The Islamic canon leg (Nasr; Al-Biruni; Ibn al-Haytham; Al-Shatibi's *Al-Muwafaqat* on the purposes of the law; Chapra on interest-free finance) follows the programme's literature map; works not available in digitised form are named in §5.

## 3. Results

**The battery's value is one number: the spread.** A stored unit is worth the import tariff minus the export rate. At 400 units/month that spread is Rs 43.59 − Rs 8.13 = **Rs 35.46 per unit**; at 700 units it is Rs 40.23; at 150 units (unprotected) Rs 26.38; at 200 units (protected) Rs 7.68. The spread, not the sunshine, decides whether a battery pays.

**Table 1. Five Larkana households, 5 kW system, November 2026 prices.**

| Household | All-in import (Rs/unit) | Value of a stored unit (Rs) | On-grid saving (Rs/yr) | Hybrid saving (Rs/yr) | On-grid payback (yr) | Hybrid payback (yr) | Marginal battery payback (yr) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100 units, unprotected | 28.04 | 19.91 | 83,874 | 98,407 | 8.94 | 10.42 | **18.92** |
| 150 units, unprotected | 34.51 | 26.38 | 93,442 | 122,325 | 8.03 | 8.38 | **9.52** |
| 200 units, protected | 15.81 | 7.68 | 81,659 | 92,868 | 9.18 | 11.04 | **24.53** |
| 400 units, unprotected | 43.59 | 35.46 | 143,210 | 195,625 | 5.24 | 5.24 | **5.25** |
| 700 units, unprotected | 48.36 | 40.23 | 211,227 | 270,693 | 3.55 | 3.79 | **4.62** |

**The crossover is 143 units/month.** Scanning consumption from 50 to 700 units, the marginal battery payback first falls to 10 years — the warranted life — at **143 units/month for unprotected households**. Below it, the battery never repays its premium inside its life (18.92 years at 100 units). For protected households there is **no crossover at all up to 700 units/month**: at 200 protected units the marginal payback is 24.53 years, more than twice the battery's life. The discontinuity is a policy artefact, not physics: the same 150-unit household pays the battery back in 9.52 years if unprotected and never if protected, because the protected tariff (Rs 13.01 base) leaves almost no spread to arbitrage.

**The battery pays for itself — as an increment, not as a system.** At 400 units/month the whole hybrid system's simple payback (5.24 years) is identical to on-grid solar alone (5.24 years), because the battery is a slower-returning increment on a fast-returning panel investment. Judged on its own increment, the battery is decisively profitable: it shifts 1,478 units a year, worth Rs 52,415 a year, against a Rs 275,000 premium — a 5.25-year payback and Rs 524,148 of lifetime value, or **Rs 1.91 back for every rupee of premium**. Over 25 years (NPV, 10% discount, battery replaced once in year 11), the hybrid beats on-grid by Rs 401,506 at 400 units, Rs 498,454 at 700 units, and Rs 71,039 at 150 units — but *loses* by Rs 165,367 at 200 protected units, where the hybrid NPV is negative (−Rs 44,229) against on-grid's +Rs 121,137.

**Sensitivity.** (i) Export rate: at Rs 11/unit the 400-unit marginal payback rises to 5.71 years; at the old net-metering rate of Rs 25.32 it rises to 10.18 years — the battery's case exists *only because* of net billing, and a return to parity pricing would erase it. (ii) Battery premium: across the observed Rs 225,000–325,000 range, the crossover moves from 117 to 169 units/month. (iii) Battery life: if the 6,000-cycle rating delivers 16 years instead of the 10-year warranty, the crossover falls to 101 units/month. (iv) Solar yield: the crossover is insensitive to yield between 4.5 and 5.5 kWh/kWp/day, because the battery's throughput is limited by night demand, not by generation — the threshold is set by the tariff ladder, not by the sun. (v) Load profile: the crossover is the most sensitive to the day/night split — at a 50% night share it rises to 172 units/month, at 70% it falls to 123, and at 30% (a household that uses most electricity while the sun shines) it rises to 247. The 143-unit headline therefore assumes a 60% night load; a household running daytime air-conditioning crosses much earlier in tariff terms but needs less storage, and the field trial must measure the split, not assume it.

**Emissions.** Using the grid factor implied by the December 2025 *Sustainable Futures* life-cycle study (8.91 tCO₂ avoided per 10.89 MWh), the 5 kW system avoids **7.5 tCO₂ per year**. The battery itself adds no generation; its climate contribution is enabling the solar energy to displace night-time grid electricity instead of being exported at a price that discourages further solar adoption.

## 4. Discussion

**Mechanism.** Net billing split one price into two, and the battery is the device that converts the cheap price into the expensive one. Every unit a household stores at midday and uses at night is a unit it does not sell for Rs 8.13 and does not buy back for Rs 43.59. The mechanism has three links, each testable: (1) the spread must exceed ~Rs 19/unit for the premium to repay in 10 years at full cycling — verified arithmetically from the notified tariff; (2) the household must have enough night demand to cycle the battery — verified by the load model, and the reason the crossover sits at 143 units rather than at the tariff boundary; (3) the battery must actually deliver ~4 kWh/day for a decade — the one link this paper cannot verify, which is why the falsifier is a field trial, not a spreadsheet.

**Comparison against the strongest incumbent.** The July 2026 Karachi study is the best available benchmark, and our results agree with it where they should and differ where the setting differs. It found full battery backup the highest-NPV configuration (~4-year payback) and net metering the lowest-LCOE one — the same ranking our NPV analysis gives at 400+ units. It also warned, as we do, that the lower export compensation "reduces the economic incentive for net-metered systems despite their technical efficiency". Where we go further: that study compared four fixed configurations for one Karachi household; we derive the *threshold* as a function of consumption and tariff category, show it is a cliff between protected and unprotected households, and show the battery's case vanishes entirely if the export rate returns to its old level. Against the PIDE brief's 7–9-year payback for an import-reliant 10 kW system, our 5.24-year figure for a 5 kW system at 400 units is shorter because the smaller system self-consumes a larger share of its output and the battery lifts self-consumption further — a sizing lesson, not a contradiction: oversizing under net billing is what destroys payback.

**What this is not.** This is a deterministic techno-economic calculation from regulated prices, not an estimate of what households actually do. No causal effect on adoption, bills, or welfare is claimed; the "natural experiment" is the regulatory switch itself, which changed the price structure for every prosumer on the same day. Realised paybacks will differ with roof shading, panel soiling (Larkana dust), inverter outages, and — most importantly — whether SEPCO settles surplus export credits quarterly as the regulations provide. At low consumption the model books large export credits (a 100-unit household exports ~8,600 units a year against near-zero imports); if settlement is delayed, low-consumption paybacks lengthen materially. That is a limitation, stated in §5, and a reason the field trial in the falsifier must meter *settled* credits, not generated units.

## 5. Limitations

1. **No metered data.** Every number is computed from tariffs and market prices, not measured in Larkana homes. The 40/60 day/night load split, the 5.0 kWh/kWp/day yield, and the 90% round-trip efficiency are assumptions; the load split is the largest uncertainty in the threshold (crossover 123–247 units/month across a 70–30% night share), and the falsifier in the Abstract is the trial that would replace them.
2. **Export-credit realisation assumed.** Surplus credits are booked at Rs 8.13/unit in full. If DISCOs delay quarterly settlement — a live complaint in 2026 reporting — low-consumption paybacks are overstated.
3. **GST and fixed charges excluded.** Including 18% GST on imports would shorten paybacks by roughly a sixth; the exclusion is conservative but the exact figure is not modelled.
4. **One battery, one size.** Only the 5 kW + 5 kWh combination is modelled. Larger batteries, second-life batteries, and time-of-use tariffs would move the threshold and are not covered.
5. **Islamic canon gap.** Nasr's *Man and Nature* was consulted in the digitised text available via the Internet Archive (per the programme's literature map). Al-Biruni's *Al-Qanun al-Mas'udi* was **not** consulted in full — no complete digitised English text was available to this run — and Al-Biruni is cited here for the *Kitab al-Hind* and the geodetic tradition, not from a fresh reading of the *Qanun*. Classical Arabic manuscripts held undigitised at the Süleymaniye, Dar al-Kutub and the British Library remain unread, as the map records.

## 6. Policy implications

**Decision-maker 1 — NEPRA.** Do not restore the old parity export rate: at Rs 25.32/unit the battery's marginal payback rises to 10.18 years and the storage incentive the 2026 reform accidentally created disappears. Instead, publish the export rate as a *floor* with a 12-month notice period for changes, so households can invest against a known spread. The Islamic principle is *maslaha* (public interest, Al-Shatibi, *Al-Muwafaqat*): regulatory certainty that lets households plan is itself a public good, and a rule changed without notice destroys it. **Falsifier for this recommendation:** if a published floor fails to raise hybrid adoption among 150–400-unit households within two years, the constraint is not price certainty but credit — see recommendation 2.

**Decision-maker 2 — Government of Sindh, Energy Department, with SEPCO.** Target any battery subsidy or financing at **unprotected households using 150–700 units/month** — the band where the battery pays — and explicitly exclude protected and lifeline households, for whom the same purchase destroys value (24.53-year payback at 200 protected units). A subsidy that ignores the tariff ladder would transfer public money to households that lose on the deal, violating *'adl* (justice: the burden must not fall on the poorest — Qur'an 4:135) and *hifz al-mal* (preservation of wealth, one of the five purposes of the law, Al-Ghazali, *Al-Mustasfa*; Al-Shatibi, *Al-Muwafaqat*). Financing must be interest-free: a conventional battery loan at interest would breach the prohibition of *riba* (Qur'an 2:275–279), so the window should be structured as diminishing *musharakah* or *murabaha* through Islamic banks (Chapra, *Islam and the Economic Challenge*, 1992). **Exact change:** a Sindh solar-plus-storage window covering the Rs 275,000 hybrid premium, first-loss-guaranteed, for unprotected 150–700-unit households, disbursed through SEPCO billing records (which already hold the consumption data that identifies the eligible band).

**Decision-maker 3 — SEPCO.** Settle surplus export credits quarterly and publish the settlement lag. Until settlement is reliable, every payback figure in this paper is an upper bound on what low-consumption households actually receive. The principle is *shura* (consultation, Qur'an 42:38): the households bearing the reform were not consulted when net metering ended, and transparent settlement data is the minimum repair.

## 7. Conclusion

NEPRA's February 2026 switch from net metering to net billing accidentally created the strongest economic case for home batteries Pakistan has ever had — and drew a sharp line through the middle of the tariff ladder. In Larkana, a 5 kW hybrid system pays its battery back in 5.25 years for a 400-unit household and in 4.62 years at 700 units, but the same battery needs 18.92 years at 100 units and 24.53 years for a protected 200-unit household. The crossover is **143 units/month** for unprotected households, and there is none for protected ones. The policy conclusion follows the arithmetic: subsidise storage for the 150–700-unit unprotected band, finance it without interest, leave protected households alone, and publish the export rate as a floor. The claim stands or falls on a 30-household metered trial — and on whether NEPRA keeps the spread that makes the battery pay.
