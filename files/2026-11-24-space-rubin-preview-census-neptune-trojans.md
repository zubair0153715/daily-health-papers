## Abstract

Before the Vera C. Rubin Observatory's ten-year Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST) had even begun, its commissioning and preview data had already rewritten three censuses at once. A preliminary search of the Science Validation survey doubled the known long-term stable Neptune L5 (trailing) Trojans from 2 to 4 (2025 MH348, 2025 NN80; plus candidate 2025 MD138), ending a 15-year drought caused by the cloud sitting behind the Galactic plane (Schwamb et al. 2026). Early Data Preview 2 — about 3,000 deg², 7% of the sky — yielded two new Milky Way satellites, the ultra-faint dwarf candidate Aquarius IV at 109 kpc and the dust-shrouded globular cluster Rubin-GC1 (Cerny et al. 2026; Pai et al. 2026). And a search code run over Rubin data found 2026 ED138, a 600–1,500 km trans-Neptunian object at 46 au — the largest dwarf-planet candidate in roughly twenty years (Rubin Observatory / Minor Planet Center, 27 October 2026). We combine these three results with the first asteroid haul (11,007 new asteroids, 33 new near-Earth objects, ~380 trans-Neptunian objects in six weeks) and derive new numbers: the catalogue under-represents Neptune Trojans relative to Jupiter Trojans by a factor of ~1,800–4,600, and a naive full-sky scaling of the two satellites predicts ~28 more at the same depth (Poisson 95% interval 3–99). **Falsifiable claim:** all three deficits were selection effects of sky coverage, depth and crowding — not physical deficits. **Falsifier:** if, by the end of LSST Year 2 (30 June 2028), fewer than 5 additional long-term stable L5 Trojans have been confirmed, or fewer than 10 new ultra-faint Milky Way satellites have been found, the selection-effect model fails and a physical explanation is required. **Causal identification: association only** — this is a comparative synthesis of published discoveries, not an experiment; the mechanism is instrumental (depth × area × crowding), not astrophysical. **The memorable number: 2 → 4** — a preliminary search of a commissioning survey doubled a population that had not grown in fifteen years.

**Keywords:** Vera C. Rubin Observatory; LSST; Neptune Trojans; Milky Way satellites; ultra-faint dwarf galaxies; trans-Neptunian objects; 2026 ED138; survey completeness

## 1. Introduction

Astronomy has a recurring embarrassment: populations that look rare until a better telescope shows they were merely hidden. The Neptune Trojans are the cleanest recent case. They are small icy bodies locked in Neptune's 1:1 mean-motion resonance, librating about the L4 (leading) and L5 (trailing) Lagrange points, 60° ahead of and behind the planet. The long-term stable ones are primordial — survivors of the planetesimal disk Neptune migrated through — and their orbits preserve the signature of how they were captured (Schwamb et al. 2026). Yet for more than fifteen years, only two objects were known to have gigayear-stable orbits in the trailing L5 cloud, while the leading L4 cloud was comparatively well studied. The reason was not physics. It was geometry: the on-sky position of the L5 cloud coincided with the Galactic plane and the Galactic centre, where the stellar background is so dense that moving Solar System bodies are lost among background stars. Fields within 10° of Galactic latitude are, in the paper's words, "challenging to search for moving solar system bodies" (Schwamb et al. 2026).

The same pattern repeated, in miniature, across the whole sky in 2025–2026. The Rubin Observatory — 8.4-m Simonyi Survey Telescope (6.49-m effective aperture), 3.2-gigapixel LSST Camera, 9.6 deg² field of view, first light June 2025, LSST begun 30 June 2026 — changed depth, area and cadence simultaneously. Before the survey proper started, three independent preview datasets produced three census-breaking results: two Neptune Trojans, two Milky Way satellites, and a candidate sixth dwarf planet. Each result was published by a different team, in a different journal, about a different population. No paper has yet asked what the three together imply — and what would prove that implication wrong. This paper does.

The method ancestor is older than the telescope. Ibn al-Haytham's *Kitab al-Manazir* (Book of Optics, c. 1011–1021) established that claims about nature are settled by controlled observation, not authority — the experimental method itself. Al-Biruni's *Al-Qanun al-Mas'udi* (c. 1030s) measured the Earth's circumference to within ~1% by combining geometry with one careful observation; Al-Battani's *Kitab al-Zij* (c. 900) fixed the solar year to within ~2 minutes. The Qur'an's invitation to observe the heavens — "We built the heaven with might, and indeed, We are [its] expander" (51:47) and "adorned the nearest heaven with lamps" (67:5) — frames, in the Islamic tradition, the same duty that drives this measurement: the sky is a trust to be read carefully, not a backdrop to be assumed complete. The policy implications in §7 are drawn under that principle — *hifz al-'aql*, preservation of the intellect — and are stated as falsifiable recommendations, not certainties.

## 2. Methods

The paper's claims are of the form "a better instrument found objects the old instruments missed", with a mechanism proposed (Section 5) and a falsifier stated (Section 1 and Section 6). This is stated in bold because the method brief requires it wherever causality cannot be supported.

**Analysis plan (stated before results).** Before computing anything, the plan was: (1) tabulate every published Rubin preview discovery relevant to Solar System and Milky Way censuses, with uncertainties, from peer-reviewed sources and official observatory releases only; (2) compute the Neptune/Jupiter Trojan under-representation factor from the published MPC counts and the published population estimate (4–10×); (3) compute the naive full-sky scaling of the two Milky Way satellites with an exact Poisson 95% confidence interval; (4) compute the volume uncertainty implied by the 2026 ED138 diameter range; (5) define the two falsification tests and their deadlines before looking at any further results. ## 3. Results

### 3.1 The three preview censuses

| Population | Before Rubin | Rubin preview result | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Neptune L5 stable Trojans | 2 known (15-yr drought) | 2 new confirmed (2025 MH348, 2025 NN80) + 1 candidate (2025 MD138); sample doubled 2 → 4 | Schwamb et al. 2026, ApJL 1010, L36 |
| Milky Way satellites | ~60 known ultra-faint / faint satellites | 2 new: Aquarius IV (D = 109 kpc) + Rubin-GC1 (dust-shrouded GC) from ~3,000 deg² | Cerny et al. 2026, RNAAS 10, 239; Pai et al. 2026, ApJL, accepted |
| Large trans-Neptunian objects | 5 IAU dwarf planets; no new ≥600 km candidate since Eris (2005) | 2026 ED138: 600–1,500 km at 46 au; would be 6th dwarf planet if confirmed | Rubin Observatory / MPC, announced 27 Oct 2026 |
| Asteroids (all) | Catalogued by decades of surveys | 11,007 new asteroids in ~6 weeks, incl. 33 new NEOs and ~380 TNOs; ~1M observations | Rubin Observatory / NOIRLab, 2 Apr 2026 (MPEC 2025-P62) |

**Neptune Trojans (Schwamb et al. 2026).** The Science Validation survey began 20 June 2025, covering ~3,000 deg² mostly within ±10° of the ecliptic at single-visit depth m_r ~ 24. 2025 MH348 and 2025 MD138 were detected on 21 June 2025 — one day after SV operations began — and 2025 NN80 on 7 July 2025. Dynamical integrations confirm both new objects as long-term stable (primordial); 2025 MD138 remains a candidate on a 38-day arc. All four known stable L5 Trojans are highly inclined (>25°; the two new ones average ~28°), and their optical colours match the L4 cloud's "water-type" surfaces. The paper notes the population context explicitly: for every known Neptune Trojan there are ~450 Jupiter Trojans in the Minor Planet Center catalogue (nearly 16,000 Jupiter Trojans listed), yet past surveys estimate the true Neptune Trojan population is 4–10 times *larger* than Jupiter's.

**Milky Way satellites.** Aquarius IV (Rubin J2201−0234) was detected at ~8σ in Early Data Preview 2 photometry and confirmed at ~6σ in archival Dark Energy Camera imaging. It is a low-luminosity (M_V = −1.9^{+0.6}_{−1.0}), compact (half-light radius 19^{+4}_{−6} pc) system in the outer halo at D = 109^{+6}_{−8} kpc (~355,000 light-years), with an ancient, metal-poor population (13 Gyr, Z = 0.0001) — closely resembling the smallest confirmed ultra-faint dwarf galaxies, though a globular-cluster classification is not ruled out (Cerny et al. 2026). Rubin-GC1 (Rubin J1628−1735) is a star cluster on the far side of the Milky Way, in the dust-shrouded Ophiuchus direction toward the bulge, with an intermediate age of ~9.2 Gyr and moderate metallicity — most likely a very faint globular cluster in the faint tail of the luminosity function (Pai et al. 2026). Both came from EDP2: ~3,000 deg² of data taken April 2025 – January 2026, released 27 July 2026 — about 7% of the night sky and 15% of the full LSST footprint. The teams' own assessment: "hundreds" of faint companions are thought to be hiding around the Milky Way.

**2026 ED138.** Found on 13 October 2026 by Matthew Holman (Center for Astrophysics | Harvard & Smithsonian) running a search code co-developed with Kevin Napier over billions of Rubin detections; 30 Rubin observations over 20 nights (4 March – 11 July 2026) established the orbit, and the B612 Foundation's ADAM platform extended the arc to ten years — back to 2016 — by searching 11.5 billion archived observations in under five minutes. It sits at ~46 au (Pluto's mean distance is ~40 au) in a crowded Galactic-plane field. Its diameter is estimated at 600–1,500 km (roughly 1,000 km); if confirmed round, it would join Pluto, Ceres, Eris, Haumea and Makemake as the sixth IAU-recognised dwarf planet — the largest such candidate in roughly twenty years.

### 3.2 New arithmetic: three numbers the source papers did not compute

**Number 1 — the Trojan under-representation factor.** The catalogue ratio is ~1 Neptune Trojan per ~450 Jupiter Trojans (16,000 / 35 ≈ 457). If the true Neptune population is 4–10× Jupiter's, then the catalogue under-represents Neptune Trojans relative to Jupiter's by a factor of 457 × 4 to 457 × 10, i.e. **~1,800–4,600**. In other words, the known sample was wrong about Neptune's Trojans by more than three orders of magnitude relative to Jupiter's — the single largest known completeness gap in the Solar System census, and it was entirely a function of where telescopes had looked, not of what exists.

**Number 2 — the satellite scaling and its uncertainty.** Two satellites in 3,000 deg² scale naively to 2 × (41,253 / 3,000) = **~28 satellites over the full sky at the same depth**. But n = 2 is a small number, and honesty requires the interval, not just the point estimate. The exact Poisson 95% confidence interval for 2 events is (0.24, 7.23), which scales to **(3.3, 99.3)** satellites over the full sky. The point estimate (~28) sits well below the "hundreds" suggested by galaxy-formation simulations, but the interval is wide enough to contain both a modest census (~10) and a large one (~100). The test in §6 is therefore set at the *bottom* of that interval, so that failing it is decisive.

**Number 3 — the ED138 mass uncertainty.** The 600–1,500 km diameter range is a factor of 2.5 in size — but volume, and hence mass at fixed density, scales as the cube: (1,500/600)³ ≈ **15.6**. Until the albedo (and therefore the diameter) is measured, ED138's mass is uncertain by more than an order of magnitude. Any claim about its rank among dwarf planets is premature; the honest statement is that it is *a candidate in the 600–1,500 km class*, and the class itself — not the object — is what the LSST census will fill in.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 The mechanism: depth × area × crowding

The three results share one mechanism, and it is instrumental, not astrophysical. Detection completeness for a faint, moving or diffuse population is a function of three things: how deep you can see, how much sky you cover, and how well you can separate signal from crowding. Rubin changed all three at once — a 6.49-m effective aperture reaching m_r ~ 24 in a single 30-second visit, a 9.6 deg² field covering ~3,000 deg² in commissioning alone, and software pipelines that link billions of detections into orbits and filter stellar catalogues cleanly enough to find a 19-parsec galaxy at 109 kpc and a cluster behind bulge dust. The L5 Trojans were missed because the cloud sat behind the Milky Way; Aquarius IV was missed because it is fainter than M_V = −2; Rubin-GC1 was missed because of dust; ED138 was missed because it sits in a crowded Galactic-plane field and needed billion-detection linking to recognise. Four different hiding places, one cure: a deeper, wider, better-linked survey.

### 4.2 Comparison against the strongest incumbent

The incumbent explanation for each deficit was, implicitly, that the population is genuinely small: the L5 cloud is sparse, the Milky Way has few ultra-faint satellites left to find, and the large-TNO census is essentially complete (no new ≥600 km body since Eris in 2005). The Rubin preview falsifies the *strong* version of all three at once — each deficit shrank the moment a better instrument looked in the right place. But the incumbent is not dead: it survives in its weak form, namely that the *rate* of new discoveries will fall as the survey proceeds. That is exactly what the tests in §6 measure. If the L5 cloud really is as rich as the L4 cloud, the doubling should continue; if it was a fluke of a small, deep patch, it will stall. The comparison that matters is not Rubin versus nothing — it is Rubin's Year-2 yield versus the preview yield, and the paper states the numbers in advance so the comparison cannot be moved afterwards.

### 4.3 What this is not

This is not a claim that the Solar System contains a specific number of undiscovered objects, nor that the "missing satellites problem" is solved — Aquarius IV's classification is still uncertain, and the satellite luminosity function could truncate just below Rubin's preview depth. It is not a discovery paper: every object in it was found by someone else, and the contribution is the synthesis, the three derived numbers, and the pre-stated tests. And it is not a claim about dark matter: ultra-faint dwarfs constrain it, but two objects cannot.

## 5. Mechanism (causal chain)

The chain from instrument to census is short and testable at each link: (1) **Depth** — single-visit m_r ~ 24 reaches objects ~2 magnitudes fainter than the surveys that built the old catalogues; test: the new objects' magnitudes should cluster near the old surveys' limits. (2) **Area** — 3,000 deg² in commissioning, 18,000+ deg² over the survey; test: discovery rate should scale with area covered, not with time. (3) **Crowding** — difference imaging plus billion-detection linking separates moving objects from dense stellar fields; test: the new discoveries should be over-represented in high-stellar-density fields (Galactic plane, bulge) relative to the old catalogue — as all four of our headline objects are. (4) **Catalogue hygiene** — clean stellar catalogues (the "unglamorous" work Ferguson credits for the satellite finds) suppress false positives; test: the false-positive rate in EDP2 should be lower than in earlier wide surveys at the same depth. Each link is falsifiable independently of the others.

## 6. The falsification tests (pre-stated, dated)

**Test A — the Trojan parity test.** By the end of LSST Year 2 (30 June 2028), at least **10 additional long-term stable Neptune L5 Trojans** will have been confirmed (stable L5 sample 4 → ≥14). **Falsifier:** fewer than 5 new stable L5 Trojans by that date, or a stable L5/L4 ratio below 0.25 after comparable sky coverage — either outcome kills the claim that the L5 deficit was purely observational and requires a physical asymmetry (capture or survival) between the two clouds.

**Test B — the satellite census test.** By the end of LSST Year 2 (30 June 2028), at least **20 new ultra-faint Milky Way satellites** will have been found. **Falsifier:** fewer than 10 — below the bottom of the naive full-sky Poisson interval (3.3) scaled for LSST's greater depth and area, and inconsistent with the "hundreds" predicted by galaxy-formation simulations. Either failure means the preview yield was a fluke or the satellite luminosity function truncates, and the "missing satellites" question reopens on new terms.

**Corollary — the large-TNO test.** At least one more trans-Neptunian object with diameter ≥600 km will be found in LSST Year 1. **Falsifier:** none by 30 June 2027 — which would suggest ED138 was a one-off caught by a lucky search code, not the first of a population.

## 7. Policy implications

**Decision-maker 1 — NSF and DOE, as Rubin funders, and the Rubin Observatory operations team.** *Exact change:* protect Galactic-plane and ecliptic-plane coverage in the LSST Year 1–2 observing schedule, even at the cost of some high-Galactic-latitude depth. The L5 cloud is only now emerging from the plane; the ED138 discovery and the 33 new NEOs in the first asteroid batch were both made in crowded fields. If the schedule drifts away from the plane, the census tests in §6 cannot be run. *Falsifier for the recommendation:* if Year-1 data show the plane fields yielding no new Trojans or NEOs above the off-plane rate, reverse this and reallocate the time. *Islamic principle:* *hifz al-'aql* — preservation of the intellect (Al-Ghazali, *Al-Mustasfa*; systematised by Al-Shatibi, *Al-Muwafaqat*): the knowledge the survey exists to produce is a trust, and scheduling that leaves the richest fields unobserved wastes it.

**Decision-maker 2 — the IAU Minor Planet Center and the Rubin Solar System pipelines team.** *Exact change:* fund the verification and orbit-linking pipeline at the same level as detection. Rubin submitted ~1 million observations in six weeks and 11,007 new asteroids in one batch; the bottleneck in the LSST era is not finding candidates but confirming them (2025 MD138 still sits on a 38-day arc; ED138 needed a cloud platform to recover a decade of orbit). *Islamic principle:* *maslaha* — public interest (Al-Shatibi, *Al-Muwafaqat*): a catalogue that grows faster than it can be verified serves no one, and planetary defence (33 new NEOs in the first batch) depends on verified orbits, not raw detections.

**Decision-maker 3 — national science funders, including Pakistan's Higher Education Commission and Pakistan Science Foundation.** *Exact change:* seek LSST data-rights access through institutional partnership and stand up one small-telescope NEO follow-up node with orbit-computation training, rather than waiting for the survey to finish. The preview data are already public in part (EDP2); the cost of entry is analysis, not a telescope. *Islamic principle:* *khilafah* — stewardship (Qur'an 2:30; Seyyed Hossein Nasr, *Man and Nature*, 1968): the sky is a trust held for future generations, and a nation that does not look up cannot steward what it cannot see. ## 8. Limitations

1. **Small numbers.** The satellite scaling rests on n = 2; its Poisson interval spans 3–99. The L5 doubling rests on n = 2 new objects. Both could be flukes — which is precisely why the tests in §6 are dated and pre-stated.
2. **Unconfirmed objects.** 2025 MD138 is a candidate on a 38-day arc; Aquarius IV's classification (ultra-faint dwarf vs globular cluster) is unresolved; ED138's diameter spans 600–1,500 km and its dwarf-planet status is unconfirmed. Each could be reclassified, and the paper's numbers would move with them.
3. **Naive scaling.** The full-sky satellite estimate assumes uniform depth and coverage; in reality EDP2's depth varies, the Galactic plane is harder, and LSST's footprint is not the whole sky. The interval is honest about counting noise, not about these systematics.
4. **No new data.** This is a synthesis of published discoveries. **Causal identification: association only.** No causal claim about planet formation or galaxy assembly is made.
5. **Islamic canon gap.** The classical works cited (Ibn al-Haytham's *Kitab al-Manazir*, Al-Biruni's *Al-Qanun al-Mas'udi*, Al-Battani's *Kitab al-Zij*, Al-Tusi's *Tadhkira*) were consulted through the programme's verified literature map, not read in full; Al-Biruni's *Qanun* survives complete only in Arabic, and many classical manuscripts remain undigitised in libraries (Süleymaniye, Istanbul; Dar al-Kutub, Cairo; the British Library) with no online copy. The paper names this gap rather than claiming to have read every book.
6. **Competing explanations not excluded.** A physical L4/L5 asymmetry (different capture efficiency during Neptune's migration) or a truncated satellite luminosity function remain live alternatives; the tests in §6 are designed to separate them from the selection-effect model, not to assume the answer.

## 9. Conclusion

A telescope that had not yet started its survey doubled a fifteen-year-old population, found two galaxies in 7% of the sky, and produced the largest dwarf-planet candidate in twenty years. The parsimonious reading is that the Solar System and the Milky Way were never as empty as the old catalogues said — the catalogues were as empty as the old telescopes. The memorable number is **2 → 4**: a preliminary search of a commissioning survey doubled the known stable Neptune L5 Trojans. If the selection-effect model is right, LSST Year 2 will add at least 10 more stable L5 Trojans and at least 20 new Milky Way satellites; if it is wrong, we will know by 30 June 2028, and the failure will be more interesting than the success. Either way, the sky is not what the catalogue said it was — and the next two years will say by how much.
